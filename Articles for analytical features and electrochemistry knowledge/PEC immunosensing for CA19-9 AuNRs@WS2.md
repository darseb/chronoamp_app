## **1. Introduction** 

CA19-9 is a mucin-type carbohydrate protein tumor marker, which is a glycolipid on the cell membrane and is the most sensitive marker for pancreatic cancer so far [1]. The normal range of CA19-9 in vivo is lower than 37 U/mL [2]. A significant increase in its content often indicates potential pancreatic cancer [3], bile duct cancer [4] or other digestive tract tumor lesions [5]. Taking the normal reference concentration (37 U/mL) as the diagnostic criterion, the positive increase in the CA19-9 concentration is closely associated with pancreatic cancer, both the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity can reach more than 95 %. Therefore, CA19-9 has been considered as a good biomarker for the diagnosis of pancreatic cancer [3]. The validation of pancreatic cancer 

prognosis through monitoring of CA19-9 levels has been explored, and demonstrating that monitoring of CA19-9 biomarkers in human serum presents a potential alternative for the prognosis of pancreatic cancer after chemotherapy as well as resection of tumors [6–8]. CA19-9 has also been confirmed as an anchor marker for pancreatic cancer early detection applications [9]. However, pancreatic cancer has no obvious symptoms and signs in the early stage, patients diagnosed with pancreatic cancer are commonly at the advanced stage [10]. Thus, the mortality of pancreatic cancer remains stubbornly high, and the 5-year survival rate is as low as 3–8 % [11]. Effective strategies for the early diagnosis of pancreatic cancer patients are essentially important for providing optimal treatments and improving survival rate. play important role in the early diagnosis of pancreatic cancer. Up to now, chemiluminescence immunoassay [12,13], radioimmunoassay [14], optical biosensors [15,16], electrochemiluminescence [17,18], electrochemical immunosensing [19,20] and photoelectrochemical biosensing [21,22] systems have been developed for the highperformance detection of CA19-9. Among them, photoelectrochemical immunosensing system has been paid much more attention due to their inherent superiorities including high sensitivity, high selectivity, ultralow background signal and easy miniaturization. 

Photoelectrochemical biosensing is a rapidly developing biological detection technology in recent years, which uses light as the excitation source and photocurrent as the detection signal. The complete separation of the detection signal from the excitation signal can provide ultralow background signal, thus showing ultra-high sensitivity towards targets. Nowadays, photoelectrochemical biosensing system has been widely employed in high-efficiency detection of bioactive molecules, ions, proteins, cells, bacteria and other fields [23,24]. Photoactive materials play crucial role in photoelectrochemical biosensing platforms due that they can supply detection signals for the quantitative determination of biomolecules and are key factors for improving sensitivity. Till now, metal-oxide semiconductors, quantum dots, MOFs, COFs and transition metal dichalcogenide including MoS2, MoSe2, WS2 have been employed as photoactive elements for developing photoelectrochemical sensing interfaces [25–29]. Transition metal dichalcogenides have been employed as active elements for improving the performances of electrochemical and photoelectrochemical sensing systems. WS2 can provide satisfactory photoelectric conversion property due to its prominent visible light absorption ability and high electron migration rate [30,31]. Furthermore, the band gap of WS2 can be transferred from indirect band gap to direct band gap through adjusting its thickness [32]. By using ionic liquid as an auxiliary reagent for exfoliating tungsten disulfite to produce WS2 nanosheets and integrating with hollow gold nanospheres, a photoelectrochemical immunosensor was prepared for determining human epididymis protein 4 [30]. Functionalizing with ionic liquid and gold nanoballs, WS2 nanosheets were successfully used for a photoelectrochemical sensing platform for 2,4,6-tribromophenol determination [33]. Gold nanoparticles were also integrated onto WS2 nanosheets surface to enhance photocurrent response for improving the photoelectrochemical immunosensing of carcinoembryonic antigen [34]. In addition, WS2 was also coupled with TiO2, CdS, MoS2, and carbon dots to enhance photoelectric conversion efficiency and thus to improve photoelectrochemical responses and sensing performances [35–38]. 

Herein, WS2 nanofilm was integrated onto gold nanorods surface to produce AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites, and thus to enhance photocurrent response. AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites were used as a photoactive element to fabricate a photoelectrochemical sensing platform. By assembling CA19-9 antibody onto AuNRs@WS2 based interface, a photoelectrochemical immunosensing platform was provided for the high-performance determination of CA19-9. Under optimized conditions, the photocurrent variations before and after the specific recognition of CA19-9 were linearly related to CA19-9 concentration in the range from 0.001 U mL[−][1 ] to 50 U mL[−][1] . A low detection limit of 0.00068 U mL[−][1 ] was obtained for CA19-9 determination. The practicability of the PEC immunosensor was confirmed by determining CA19-9 in clinical serum samples, and demonstrating its potential applications in diagnosis. 

## **2. Materials and methods** 

## _2.1. Reagents and apparatus_ 

Chloroauric acid, cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide, sodium borohydride, silver nitrate, glutaraldehyde, and ascorbic acid were purchased from (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (China, Shanghai). Hydroquinone was supplied by Wuhan Xinshenshi Chemical Technology 

Co., Ltd. (China, Wuhan). Bovine serum albumin (BSA) and L-cysteine were bought from Shanghai Ruji Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. (China, Shanghai). CA19-9 and anti-CA19-9 were supplied by Shanghai Linc-Bio Science Co. LTD (China, Shanghai). Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) was purchased from Shanghai Bioss Biotechnology Co., LTD (China, Shanghai). Human serum albumin (HSA) was bought from Shanghai Pufei Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China, Shanghai). Immunoglobulin was purchased from Shanghai solarbio Bioscience & Technology Co., LTD (China, Shanghai). Glycine was supplied by Shanghai Shiyi Chemical Reagent Co., LTD (China, Shanghai). All other chemicals were of analytical grade and were used without any purification. 

Transmission electron microscopic (TEM) images were obtained from a FEI Tecnai G2 20S-TWIN instrument (FEICompany, Netherlands) at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. XPS analysis was performed on a MULT1LAB2000 X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS) (VG, USA). Scanning electron microscopy images were recorded on a JEOL-6700F instrument (JEOL Ltd, Japan) with the electron beam voltage of 15.0 kV. Electrochemical measurements were performed with CHI 660D electrochemical workstation (Chenhua Corp., Shanghai, China). A conventional three-electrode system was employed with a glassy carbon electrode (ø = 3 mm) as a substrate working electrode, a Pt wire auxiliary electrode and a saturated calomel reference electrode (SCE). A white light LED lamp was used as the excitation light source. All the photocurrent measurements were obtained at a constant potential of 0 V (vs. SCE) in a 0.1 M pH 7.0 phosphate buffer containing 0.1 M ascorbic acid (AA) at room temperature. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was measured in a 0.1 M KCl solution containing 5.0 mM K3[Fe(CN)6]/K4[Fe(CN)6] (1:1). The frequency was controlled over the range of 0.1 Hz to 100 kHz with the amplitude of 5 mV s[−][1] . 

## _2.2. Preparation of gold nanorods_ 

0.25 mL HAuCl4 solution (0.01 mol L[−][1] ) was thoroughly mixed with 10 mL cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide solution (0.1 mol L[−][1] ) to obtain an orange mixture solution. The newly prepared NaBH4 solution (0.06 mL, 0.01 mol L[−][1] ) was quickly introduced with stirring, and the color of the solution turned to be light brown. After being stored at 30[◦] C for 2 h under dark place, brown gold nano-seeds were obtained and stored at 4[◦] C for further use. 

1.5 mL of HAuCl4 solution (0.01 mol L[−][1] ) was mixed with 28.5 mL of CTAB solution (0.1 mol L[−][1] ), and then 0.45 mL of AgNO3 solution (0.01 mol L[−][1] ) was added and stirred for 30 s to mix homogeneously. After mixing, 1.5 mL of hydroquinone solution (0.1 mol L[−][1] ) was added and mixed to produce a colorless solution. Subsequently, 0.6 mL of the above-prepared gold seeds solution was added and stirred for 30 s to mix homogeneously. After mixing, the mixture was kept at 30[◦] C for 24 h under dark to produce gold nanorods. CTAB was thoroughly removed through centrifugation with ultra-pure water washing. The obtained AuNRs were dispersed with ultra-pure water to give a solution at the concentration of ~0.006 mol L[−][1 ] and stored at 4[◦] C for further use. 

## _2.3. Preparation of AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites_ 

1.5 mL of the above-prepared gold nanorods dispersed solution was mixed with 3 mL of cysteine solution (0.1 mol L[−][1] ). After being diluted to 10 mL with ultrapure water, and keeping at room temperature for 12 h, free cysteine was removed through centrifugation with ultra-pure water washing. Then, 0.01154 g of NaWO4⋅2H2O was added and diluted to 17.5 mL with ultrapure water. The mixture was transferred to a polytetrafluoroethylene hydrothermal autoclave reactor (25 mL) and heated to 180[◦] C for 12 h. After being cooled to room temperature, the precipitate was collected through centrifugation and washing with ultrapure water to provide AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites.

## _2.4. Fabrication of photoelectrochemical immunosensor for CA19-9_ 

A glassy carbon electrode was polished with alumina suspension, and successively being cleared in HNO3 solution (1:1), ethanol, and ultrapure water with ultrasonication. AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites dispersed solution (6.0 μL) was coated onto the cleared GCE surface, and dried under 35[◦] C to give an AuNRs@WS2/GCE interface. Then, 6.0 μL of anti-CA19-9 solution (Ab1, 10 μg mL[−][1] ) was introduced onto the AuNRs@WS2/GCE surface, after assembling and washing with phosphate buffer solution (0.01 mol⋅L[−][1] , pH 7.4) to produce an Ab1/ AuNRs@WS2/GCE. After reacting with glutaraldehyde produced from a glutaraldehyde solution (5 %) for 5 min and then incubating into a BSA solution (0.25 %) 30 min for a blocking reaction. Subsequently, thoroughly washing and dried to provide the photoelectrochemical immunosensing system, Ab1(BSA)/AuNRs@WS2/GCE. Schematics for the fabrication of the AuNRs@WS2 based photoelectrochemical immunosensor for CA19-9 was shown in Scheme 1. 

## _2.5. Electrochemical measurements_ 

All photoelectrochemical measurements were performed by using a three-electrode system on a CHI660D electrochemical workstation. A glassy carbon electrode before or after modification was used as working electrode, a Pt wire electrode was used as counter electrode, a saturated calomel electrode was used as reference electrode. The i-t curve of each interface was measured in a phosphate buffer solution (0.1 mol L[−][1] , pH 7.4) containing 0.1 mol L[−][1 ] ascorbic acid under the illumination of a white-light LED lamp (5 W). The working electrode potential was controlled at 0 V ( _vs_ . SCE). Electrochemical alternating current impedance spectroscopy was carried out in a KCl solution containing 0.5 mmolL[−][1 ] K3Fe(CN)6/K4Fe(CN)6 in the frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 1.0 × 10[5 ] Hz under the initial potential at the equilibrium potential. 

## **3. Results and discussion** 

## _3.1. Characterizations of AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites_ 

## _3.1.1. Morphological characterizations_ 

Transmission electron microscopy was employed to characterize gold nanorods and AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites respectively. As shown in Fig. 1A, rod-like gold nanoparticles can be observed clearly with an average length of ~50 nm and an average diameter of ~14 nm. As WS2 nanofilm was integrated onto gold nanorods surface, the morphology changes significantly, and turns to be irregular rod-like structure (Fig. 1B). As been magnified (Fig. 1C), a nanofilm can be seen clearly on 

the gold nanorod surface with a distinct interface, indicating the successful preparing AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites. The thickness of WS2 nanofilm on gold nanorods surface will influence of the photoelectric conversion efficiency of AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites. If WS2 nanofilm is too thin, no enough photoactive material can be employed for generating electron-hole pairs, the photocurrent response perhaps will be very weak. However, if WS2 nanofilm is too thick, poor conductivity of WS2 will retard the transfer of photogenerated electrons and holes, thus also leading to poor photocurrent response. Therefore, suitable thickness of WS2 nanofilm is necessary for obtaining the perfect photocurrent response. However, it is very difficult to control the thickness of WS2 nanofilm precisely. Controlling the thickness of WS2 nanofilm and the influence on the photocurrent response will be investigated in future work. Distribution of elements in AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites was also characterized by using EDS mapping. As shown in Fig. 2, the uniform distribution of Au, S and W elements in AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites demonstrates the successful preparation. 

## _3.1.2. Characterizations with XPS and XRD_ 

Elemental compositions of AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites were characterized by high-resolution XPS. As shown in Fig. 3A, two obvious peaks located at 35.79 and 37.94 eV are ascribe to W4f7/2 and W4f5/2 levels, respectively. For the XPS characterization of S atom, Fig. 3B, peaks located at 161.05 eV and 162.21 eV are attributed to the S2p3/2 and S2p1/2 levels. The XPS characterization of Au was shown in Fig. 3C. Peaks located at 83.83 eV and 87.47 eV are corresponding to Au4f7/2 and Au4f5/2 levels. X-Ray diffraction was employed to characterize the crystal structure of AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites. Four peaks located at 13.9[◦] , 29.6[◦] , 37.8[◦] and 50.4[◦] , shown in Fig. 3D, are ascribed to (0 0 2), (0 0 4), (0 0 6) and (1 0 5) planes of WS2 crystals. Meanwhile, peaks corresponding to the (1 1 1), (2 0 0), (2 2 0) and (3 1 1) planes of gold crystals are observed at 37.99[◦] , 44.5[◦] , 64.6[◦] and 77.7[◦] , respectively. All results from the characterizations of XPS and XRD demonstrate the successful preparation of AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites. 

## _3.1.3. Photoelectrochemical characterization of AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites_ 

The photoelectrochemical properties of AuNRs and AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites were characterized with chronoamperometry in a phosphate buffer solution containing 0.1 mol L[−][1 ] ascorbic acid. As shown in Fig. 4a, under the illumination of white light, AuNRs presents good photoelectric conversion property. As WS2 nanofilm was integrated onto AuNRs surface, the photocurrent response increases significantly (Fig. 4b), meaning WS2 nanofilm possesses excellent photoelectric conversion efficiency. The enhancement on the photocurrent mainly due to the semiconductor property of WS2 nanofilm which can be excited by white light to produce electron-hole pairs [31]. Photogenerated holes can react with ascorbic acid to separate electronhole pairs to generate photocurrent response. In addition, the local surface plasmon resonance effect and good conductivity of gold nanorods can also promote the photoconversion efficiency of AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites, thus to enhance the photocurrent response [39,40]. Therefore, AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites can be a good candidate as photoactive element for the fabrication of photoelectrochemical sensing platform.

## _3.3. Optimization of experimental conditions_ 

Experimental conditions including ascorbic acid concentration, incubation time and incubation temperature were optimized for obtaining high performances for determining CA19-9. The photocurrent variation of a AuNRs@WS2/GCE with ascorbic acid concentration in the range from 0 to 0.2 mol L[−][1 ] was shown in Fig. 6A. In the concentration range from 0 to 0.1 mol L[−][1] , the photocurrent response increases significantly. As ascorbic acid concentration increases to higher than 0.1 mol L[−][1] , the photocurrent response decreases gradually. Therefore, ascorbic acid concentration at 0.1 mol L[−][1 ] was selected for further photoelectrochemical measurements. The incubation time for the specific recognition of CA19-9 onto the immunosensing platform was investigated and shown in Fig. 6B. As the incubation time varied from 10 to 25 min, the photocurrent variation increases gradually and tends to be stable due to almost all bioactive sites have been reacted with CA19-9. Thus, 25 min should be the optimized incubation time for biorecognition of CA19-9 on the photoelectrochemical immunosensing interface. Incubation temperature will influence the biorecognition activity of anti-CA19-9 towards CA19-9, and thus influencing the sensing performances of the photoelectrochemical immunosensor for determining CA19-9. As shown in Fig. 6C, as the incubation temperature varying from 20[◦] C to 35[◦] C, the photocurrent variation increases gradually, and conversely decrease in the temperature range from 35[◦] C to 45[◦] C. Therefore, 35[◦] C was selected as the optimized incubation temperature for determining CA19-9 with the developed photoelectrochemical immunosensor.

## **4. Conclusion** 

Through in situ assembling, WS2 nanofilm was integrated onto gold nanorods surface to prepare AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites. AuNRs@WS2 nanocomposites possess perfect photoelectric conversion efficiency, thus were used as a photoactive element for fabricating a photoelectrochemical sensing platform to immobilize anti-CA19-9 to construct a immunosensor for determining CA19-9. The AuNRs@WS2 based photoelectrochemical immunosensor provides good sensitivity, selectivity and accuracy towards CA19-9. The photoelectrochemical immunosensor was successfully employed for assaying CA19-9 in real serum samples with satisfactory accuracy, thus it has the potential to be used in disease diagnosis.
