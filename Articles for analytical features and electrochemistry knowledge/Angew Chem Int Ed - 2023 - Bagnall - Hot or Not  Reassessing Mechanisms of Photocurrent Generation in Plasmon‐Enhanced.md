## **Hot or Not? Reassessing Mechanisms of Photocurrent Generation in Plasmon-Enhanced Electrocatalysis** 

_Andrew J. Bagnall, Sagar Ganguli, and Alina Sekretareva*_

**Abstract:** It is now widely accepted that certain effects arising from localised surface plasmon resonance, such as enhanced electromagnetic fields, hot carriers, and thermal effects, can facilitate electrocatalytic processes. This newly emerging field of research is commonly referred to as plasmon-enhanced electrocatalysis (PEEC) and is attracting increasing interest from the research community, particularly regarding harnessing the high energy of hot carriers. However, this has led to a lack of critical analysis in the literature, where the participation of hot carriers is routinely claimed due to their perceived desirability, while the contribution of other effects is often not sufficiently investigated. As a result, correctly differentiating between the possible mechanisms at play has become a key point of contention. In this review, we specifically focus on the mechanisms behind photocurrents observed in PEEC and critically evaluate the possibility of alternative sources of current enhancement in the reported PEEC systems. Furthermore, we present guidelines for the best experimental practices and methods to distinguish between the various enhancement mechanisms in PEEC. 

## _**1. Introduction**_ 

In recent years, there has been a lot of interest in the photoelectrochemistry of so-called plasmonic metals (Cu, Au, and Ag). It has been proposed that combining the localised surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) effects of these metals with applied potential bias can improve the efficiency of their electrocatalytic reactions.[[1–4]] This newly emerging field of research is commonly referred to as plasmonenhanced electrochemistry or electrocatalysis (PEEC), or plasmon-assisted electrochemistry. 

While the photoelectrochemistry of metals has recently received renewed interest, research into the phenomena behind light’s interaction with metal surfaces at metal/ electrolyte interfaces dates back to 1839, when Becquerel observed the appearance of an electric current upon illumination of one of two identical electrodes immersed in dilute acids.[[5]] Extensive studies on electron photoemission from the metal/electrolyte interface have been conducted, and various theories explaining the mechanism of electron photoemission have been proposed.[[6]] Investigations have been conducted on different metals, particularly Pt and Hg, employing high-intensity light sources, predominantly in the near-UV range of the spectrum, due to the high work functions of these metals. However, there are also reports where photoemission from plasmonic metals like Ag, Au, and Cu was explored.[[7]] 

In light of this review, it is especially important to mention two works. The nature of enhancement by hot carriers and via photothermal effects should fundamentally differ in terms of their ability to affect reaction selectivity and kinetics. ## _**2. Interband vs. Intraband Generation of Hot Carriers**_ 

The Fermi level of noble metals sits within a partially filled conduction band ( _sp_ band). A low-dispersed filled valence _d_ -band exists close to the Fermi level (Figure 2C).[[23]] When photons are absorbed by metals, electronic transitions occur while the total momentum is conserved. In the case of 

interband transitions, where transitions occur between the _d-_ and _sp-_ bands, this conservation requirement is naturally met when the energy surpasses the interband threshold energies (2.06 eV and 3.09 eV for Au and Ag, respectively). However, for intraband transitions ( _sp_ - _sp_ ), additional pathways for momentum conservation are necessary.[[24,25]] The non-radiative decay of plasmon resonance in nanomaterials is achieved through Landau damping, which is a purely quantum mechanical process.[[26]] It involves the complete transfer of plasmon energy to generate a single electronhole pair within the nanomaterial, occurring within a timeframe of 1–100 fs after plasmonic excitation. Interband transitions naturally conform to the momentum conservation criteria and are thus permitted. On the other hand, intraband transitions are typically considered forbidden in natural conditions. However, this prohibition can be overcome by the presence of surface plasmon polariton (SPP) modes with finite dimensions. When the effective size of the SPP mode within the metal is less than half the wavelength of the incident light, the surplus momentum carried by the surface plasmon facilitates intraband transitions. This process, known as as Landau damping, is particularly pronounced when numerous intraband states are readily available to make the transition.[[24]] The distribution of carriers at this stage is purely non-thermal; they are referred to as “hot-carriers” due to their high energies. Subsequently, these hot-carriers engage in electron-electron scattering, redistributing their energy and resulting in a Fermi-Diraclike distribution with a significantly increased effective electron temperature within a timescale of 100 fs–1 ps.[[27]] During this timeframe, their interaction with phonons intensifies, leading to equilibration with the lattice and an increase in lattice temperature. Eventually, this excess heat is dissipated into the surroundings. 

Recent literature presents differing perspectives on the catalytic role of charge carriers generated through interband and intraband transitions. In intraband transitions, when electrons near the Fermi edge are excited, their energy in the excited state significantly exceeds that achievable through thermal or interband excitations. Similarly, when electrons far from the Fermi edge are excited, the energy of the as-generated holes is lower than that achievable through conventional methods (Figure 2C). Consequently, both hot holes and electrons generated from intraband transitions exhibit higher reactivity and can contribute to catalysis if properly harvested.[[28]] Conversely, during interband transitions, hot electrons are created in the vicinity of the Fermi level and, as a result, lack sufficient energy to overcome the energy barrier for catalytic reactions. Although the hole generated within the _d_ -band possesses high energy (Figure 2C), its lower velocity due to the higher effective mass in the _d_ -band compared to the _sp_ -band should prevent it from reaching the surface.[[25,28]] Therefore, hot-carriers generated from interband transitions have traditionally been considered inefficient for catalysis. However, several research groups have recently presented a different perspective, indicating that interband charge carriers serve as the primary component in plasmonic catalysis, with minimal participation of intraband hot-carriers.[[29,30]] Kamarudheen

## _**3. Hot Carrier vs. Thermal Effects**_ 

Recent literature shows an ongoing debate regarding the relative contributions of hot-carriers and temperature in PEEC.[[31]] As has been pointed out, there is a significant difference (on the order of 10[3] ) between the typical rates of charge transfer steps in chemical reactions at an unbiased electrode, i.e. at open circuit potential (OCP) and the rate of decay of hot-carriers, which severely limits the possible involvement of hot-carriers in electrocatalysis. By adjusting the applied potential, the reaction rate on an electrode can be modulated. For instance, the time required for the conversion of one proton on an Au electrode at OCP is 5 μs, but decreases to 17 fs at an overpotential of 1 V (estimated using the Butler–Volmer equation with a charge transfer coefficient of 0.5 and an exchange current density of 10[6] A/ cm[2] ).[[32]] While the applied overpotential may increase the ability of photo-generated hot-carriers to participate, it will also increase electrocatalytic dark currents, which will dominate the overall current response of the system. At the same time, local heating of the nanostructures can also facilitate electrochemical reactions by facilitating either heterogeneous charge transfer or mass transport of reactants and products.[[33][34]] 

Researchers have employed various experimental strategies and methods to discern the individual roles of hot charge carriers and thermal effects. In this section, we will summarise recent approaches in this direction, analyse the results and corresponding discussions, and provide insights from both the existing literature and experiments conducted in our research group. 

## _**3.1. Measuring and Controlling Temperature: At the Surface vs. in Solution**_ 

In many cases thermal effects are unduly dismissed on the basis that either a fixed temperature heating bath was 

employed or no temperature change was detected in the bulk solution.[[35,36]] Nonetheless, some studies include reasonably extensive tests to measure the increase in solution temperature from room temperature (RT) after illumination, as well as the effect of externally varying the initial solution temperature across multiple values.[[37–49]] 

In some cases, a comparison of the catalytic currents in the dark for different initial solution temperatures against one or more illumination power densities (with the solution initially at RT) was made to estimate how much of a temperature increase corresponded to each illumination power density.[[42–49]] From our own studies on 20 nm diameter gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) dropcast on highly ordered pyrolytic graphite (HOPG) electrodes, we observed that an illumination power density of 325 mWcm[2] at approximately the absorption maximum resulted in the same current enhancement as a 5.3 °C temperature increase of the solution temperature, while also demonstrating by comparison with a transparent ITO (indium tin oxide-coated glass) electrode that the overwhelmingly dominant factor in this enhancement was in fact the heating effect due to light absorption by the HOPG electrode substrate itself, a process which is often overlooked (Section 3.5).[[21,50]] 

To be meaningful for the charge carriers in the electrode, measuring solution temperature implicitly relies upon there being a negligible temperature gradient between the electrode surface and the surrounding solution, requiring very rapid heat exchange between them, while also ignoring the consequences of uneven heating on the electrode surface under illumination. These assumptions are often flawed: when considering studies that specifically measure the surface temperature by other methods, often much stronger heating is seen at the electrode surface than in the bulk solution, across even the wide variety of systems and conditions reported. 

Thermocouples have been used in several reports to estimate surface temperatures (Figure 3A–C). For example, for a system of silver nanoparticles on graphene for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) under a laser power density of 28.3 Wcm[2] ,[[51]] a fluctuation in temperature within only 0.3 °C over 5 minutes was measured by thermocouple ‘near’ the electrode’s surface, despite the very high light power density, but the exact temperature of the surface itself was not reported. Whereas, for a system of AuNPs on glassy carbon for the reduction of nitrogen to ammonia under a laser power density of 5.2 Wcm[2] ,[[41]] a temperature increase in the bulk electrolyte medium from RT to around 30 °C was measured by the thermocouple ‘near’ the electrode’s surface. Critically, this was significantly lower than the surface temperature increase to around 40 °C measured for the same system when the thermocouple was in direct physical contact with the electrode immediately after irradiation. This 10 °C (about 2×) difference arising only from the positioning of the thermocouple exemplifies the significant possible magnitude of temperature gradients and the associated risks of underestimating temperature increases.

## _**3.2. Electrochemical Approaches to Distinguish Hot-carriers from Thermal Effects**_ 

A complementary way to gauge the relative contribution of photothermal effects against plasmon-generated hot-carriers and other ‘fast’ effects (the plasmoelectric effect and electromagnetic field enhancement which are discussed in detail in Section 4) is to extrapolate how much of an induced photocurrent is due to each phenomenon. This is in principle possible when _i–t_ or _i–V_ curves have been recorded with onoff switching of the light source, as the rate of enhancement or decay to a baseline should be fundamentally dependent on the distinctive nature of its source (thermal, hot-carriers, or other). As it has been first demonstrated by Zhan et al. the photocurrent response in _i–t_ curves consists of two components: a rapid response current (IRC) and a slow response current (ISC), corresponding, respectively, to photoelectronic (excited carriers) and photothermal (local heating) effects.[[59]] Thus, by recording _i–t_ curves, the relative contributions of photothermal effects and hot carriers can be estimated. (Figure 4A,B).

## _**3.3. Power Dependence**_ 

A commonly used method to differentiate between contribution of hot-carriers and thermal effects involves analysing the photo-induced catalytic response/photocurrent under varying illumination power. When illumination power increases, both temperature and the number of generated hotcarriers increase linearly.[[31,55,67]] However, both reaction kinetics and diffusion coefficients have nonlinear relationships with temperature, following the Arrhenius relationship.[[34]] Hence, if the photo-induced catalytic response/photocurrent increases linearly with illumination intensity, it suggests that the catalytic enhancement is primarily due to hot-carriers. Conversely, if an exponential (nonlinear) relationship is observed, it indicates that temperature enhancement is the main contributor. Several studies have employed this approach, and in cases where a linear relationship was observed, enhancements have been readily attributed solely to participation of hot-carriers.[[68,69]] 

In this context, we highlight a recent analysis conducted by Dubi et al. on four such previous reports.[[20]] The authors developed a first-principles theory, which predicts that only a small fraction of the total illumination power generates hot-carriers (efficiency of hot-carrier generation is 10[10] to 10[7] for the intensities on the order of hundreds of mW/cm[2] typically used in measurements), while the rest only heats the nanostructures and their surroundings. A temperatureshifted Arrhenius law was formulated to reanalyse the experimental data from the four works, all of which assumed the involvement of hot-carriers in catalytic enhancement.[[70–73]] Their analysis revealed that the powerdependent photocurrent enhancements reported in these works could be fitted with the temperature-shifted Arrhenius law. Careful scrutiny of the plotted data further indicates that significant temperature increases of several hundred degrees would often be required to clearly observe the nonlinearity. Previous studies by Maley et al. and our own experiments have also shown that the temperature increases in certain electrochemical systems illuminated with laser intensities below 1 W/cm[2] are typically less than 

10 K.[[18,21,50]] For such small temperature differences, it becomes challenging to discern the exponential relationship, and, in most cases, the data appears to follow a linear trend.[[74]] Consequently, one must exercise caution when attributing photo-induced enhancement exclusively to hotcarriers based solely on a linear photocurrent-illumination power relationship. 

Roughly half the papers surveyed here did not report investigating the light power density dependence of their plasmonic enhancement effects.[[38–41,47,52,58,58,75–86]] A few papers only tested power density effects in a very limited way.[[43,75,87]] Nonetheless, there are examples of strong linear dependence between photocurrent and power density,[[35,42,48,88,89]] but also examples of linear reductions in Tafel slopes and required overpotentials,[[37,44]] which may rather imply an exponential relationship of photocurrent itself to power density. There are also examples of systems where a linear trend is tenuously identified, but with a noticeable upwards[[64,90]] or downwards[[91,92]] curvature across plots of photocurrent against power density, and examples which observe a super-linear trend across their full range of illumination powers,[[36,45,49]] which might imply a significant photothermal role in some cases. Indeed, Wang et al. plotted their peak currents of ammonia oxidation catalysed by plasmonic Pt-Au nanoparticles on a logarithmic scale, obtaining a linear fit, indicating a clear exponential dependence on illumination power density, which fitted with their modelling of the process, whereby LSPR excitation energy contributed to overcoming activation energy barriers.[[49]] Hence, when an exponential relationship is suspected, testing a logarithmic scale on the _y_ -axis is called for, as it can prove informative. 

As a final note, models based on a power-dependent photo-plasmonic effect on activation energy barriers due to carriers tunnelling to the adsorbates have been proposed by Zhou et al.[[73]] and Rej et al.[[93]] as a way to distinguish hotcarrier effects versus photothermal ones, but critical analysis by Sivan et al. has raised questions regarding the physicality of this model, as wide ranges of values can be used for certain parameters and still obtain an apparently strong fit.[[53]] Instead, Sivan et al. suggested spatial temperature non-uniformity and underestimation as the main reasons that the activation energies appeared to have been lowered. Indeed, a direct hot-carrier effect might be better understood as a super-thermal increase in the population of carriers/molecules that can overcome the thermodynamic activation barrier, rather than in terms of separate ‘dark’ and ‘light’ activation barriers. Per Sivan et al. and statistical thermodynamics,[[94]] increased temperature (which can arise from increasing light source power) will have an exponential effect on reaction rates, meaning plots of _ln k_ vs. _T[1]_ will also show a linear correlation for photothermal contributions. Consequently, observing this relationship cannot by itself distinguish between hot-carrier and photothermal mechanisms.

## _**3.4. Wavelength Dependence**_ 

The wavelength dependence of catalytic enhancements has been invoked as evidence for hot-carrier involvement, although there are conflicting arguments regarding what kind of dependence should be expected, as the mechanisms of hot-carrier generation and enhancement processes are still debated (Section 2), and there have been different observations in different reports. Firstly, we note that observing a wavelength dependence which mostly matches the absorption spectrum of the plasmonic material[[11,70]] is problematic as evidence of non-thermal effects, as any photothermal effects should, in principle, also correlate directly to how much light is absorbed. Notwithstanding, in the literature, many studies observe this kind of direct correlation of enhancement to absorbance at each wavelength, which may equally be indicative of photothermal effects _or_ hot-carrier participation, so this does not itself enable a definitive distinction.[[35–37,40,42–45,48,58,76,81,88,90,91]] 

However, in other cases, the enhancement effect does not trivially follow the absorption spectrum of the photoelectrode. On one hand, it has been claimed by Rodio et al. that stronger enhancements may be observed for some systems when illuminated with wavelengths longer than the LSPR absorption maximum,[[92]] because local-field enhancement from LSPR is disproportionately stronger at longer wavelengths. If local-field effects are the main contributing factor, rather than hot-carriers or heating, this would rely on the specific electronic structure of the adsorbed reactants. However, there do not appear to be any examples where this effect has been demonstrated for PEEC yet, and it might generally be insignificant (see Section 4.1.). 

On the other hand, a greater enhancement by illumination with shorter wavelengths than the LSPR peak has been proposed to be a consequence of the participation of hotcarriers generated from interband transitions (see Section 2).[[29,30]] There are reports of observing this kind of dependence. For example, in a broadband light-harvesting system for water oxidation composed of CoO-decorated Au nanorods on ITO, Ghosh et al. reported substantial differences in the incident photon-to-current conversion efficiencies of different wavelength ranges, from 0.17% at 410– 650 nm down to 0.03% at 850–1700 nm around the actual LSPR peak ( ~~] 1000 nm).[[64]] Whereas, in the study of Wu et al. on MXenes, femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy showed a wavelength dependence also in the recorded kinetic traces attributed to LSPR-generated hot electrons, implying that shorter wavelengths generated longer-lived and more energetic hot electrons than wavelengths closer to the LSPR peak ( ~~] 800 nm), decreasing the required overpotential and increasing the Faradaic efficiency.[[44]] 

However, there are also reports where enhancements dramatically fall at wavelengths outside the LSPR band, and in some cases control experiments suggest photothermal effects. Rodrigues et al. reported a wavelength-dependent change in required overpotentials for the HER from a series of Au-Rh nanoflowers on a glassy carbon disk, where the wavelength closest to the LSPR absorption maximum led to 

a significantly greater decrease in overpotential and increase in current than shorter wavelengths, while longer wavelengths notably induced almost no enhancement at all.[[79]] The lesser, but still apparent, effect of the shorter wavelength was attributed to a tail in the Rh LSPR excitation band and interband transitions in Au. However, applying the system to the carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO2RR), shorter wavelengths achieved better enhancement in some cases,[[95]] implying that interband transitions might selectively assist that reaction. Either way, the authors attribute enhancement to the successful transfer of hot electrons photogenerated in the Au-core to the Rhshell, which then catalyse the reactions of interest. However, in these studies, current changes in the chronoamperograms from switching the light source on/off are dominated by slow components (on the order of seconds). As discussed in Section 3.2., slow changes in photocurrent are a strong indicator of photothermal effects, so this conflicts with the claims of hot-carrier involvement in these examples of plasmonic particles on carbon-based electrodes. 

In the case of Pd-Au hetero-nanoraspberries studied by Wang et al. for ethanol oxidation, a very low illumination power density of only 2.12 mWcm[2] was applied and a stark wavelength dependence could be observed.[[83]] When illuminated with a wavelength near the LSPR peak, a significant enhancement of the catalytic activity by the Au component was observed in cyclic voltammetry, accompanied by a significant decrease in the catalytic activity by the Pd component, whereas the effects of illumination with blue light were almost negligible, even though the heating effect from each wavelength may be expected to be comparable from the absorbance spectra for the electrode assembly, so this may be a clearer example of possible non-thermal effects. 

Overall, which processes occur and how much they contribute depends on the system, in particular the electronic structures of both the plasmonic material and adsorbed reactants. Hence, wavelength dependence cannot provide an unambiguous diagnosis of hot-carrier involvement, unless the system is already well-understood.

## _**3.6. Selectivity**_ 

When the reaction selectivity is altered by illumination (versus trivial heating), a negligible role of hot-carriers is less probable. At least some hot-carriers may participate in catalysis, and/or some electric field/plasmoelectric effects could occur, encouraging certain reaction pathways over others.[[40,98]] Nonetheless, such effects might not always account for the majority of the observed photocurrent enhancement, which may remain predominantly photothermal, due to hot-carriers’ short lifetimes and unhindered Fermi level reequilibration in these systems. Importantly, temperature effects on selectivities are also often significant and can sometimes be harnessed towards desirable products, according to the entropic/enthalpic components of the free energies of possible reaction pathways.[[98]] Generally, only plasmonic effects which enhance the desired reaction at the intended catalytic component have been reported in literature, but in a couple of systems for alcohol oxidation LSPR effects apparently result in the intended chromophore component (AuNPs) becoming more active, while the intended catalyst component (Pd-nanopetals) interestingly becomes less active.[[77,83]] 

One would expect that plasmonic effects cannot always have desirable effects on selectivity in every system, but reports regarding undesired consequences of plasmonic effects in electrocatalysis (e.g. enhancing side-reactions) are rare so far. Such results, even if apparently negative, could be informative for understanding and mitigating unforeseen side-effects of plasmonic enhancement. 

Moreover, relatively few studies have looked into selectivity effects of PEEC in barrierless systems, as most only focus on enhancing a single reaction without emphasis on mitigating side-reactions, even though this has been promoted as one of the key potential advantages of hotcarrier involvement.[[98]] Nonetheless, modifying catalytic selectivity in the CO2RR, as one example, has naturally been a significant topic of interest and has been quite 

extensively investigated in systems based on semiconductors assisted by plasmonic effects.[[99–101]] 

Out of the barrierless electrocatalytic systems reported, a few consider the possibility to prevent carbon monoxide or other side-products from poisoning and passivating surfaces and hindering alcohol oxidation or hydrogen evolution.[[48,78,80,83,88,102]] The study of Pang et al. on gold nanohole arrays for the methanol oxidation reaction[[88]] aimed to capitalise on an improved CO poisoning tolerance known for related systems,[[102]] however, they still ended up with some formic acid side-product. Some of these studies claim that LSPR effects help to reduce or undo passivation processes, observing that currents fall with each cycle in cyclic voltammetry in the dark but not under illumination.[[78,83]] However, unambiguous proof of the exact process and whether it involves hot-carriers is lacking, as beneficial structural effects from alloying different noble metals are also often credited. 

In a few cases, condition-dependent selectivity effects seem to indicate significant non-thermal processes behind plasmonic enhancement. As discussed previously, Yang et al. studied the selective oxidation of _p_ -aminothiophenol to _p,p_ ’-dimercaptoazobenzene as a molecular probe attached to the surface of Au-core Pt-shell nanoparticles.[[43]] SERS indicated that this specific reaction did _not_ take place on the surfaces of thicker-shelled nanoparticles, whose LSPR was blocked. Lin et al. studied various Ag-Pt nanocages for which hotcarriers were claimed to suppress the formation of hydrogen peroxide side-product from the ORR.[[40]] Rotating ring-disk electrode experiments revealed that illumination with a wavelength near the LSPR peak resulted in a sharp immediate decrease in proportion of peroxide produced by 

4% for the most plasmonically-active bimetallic nanoparticles, but upon excitation with double that wavelength no change in peroxide yield was observed. ## _**4. Alternative Mechanisms**_ 

Besides thermal effects and hot-carriers, several other possible contributors to the photocurrents observed on plasmonic nanostructures under illumination have been discussed in the literature. 

## _**4.1. Enhanced Electromagnetic Fields**_ 

Enhanced electromagnetic fields near nanoparticles’ surfaces can enable plasmon-induced resonance energy transfer (PIRET).[[104–106]] Because plasmons have large dipole moments as a result of their interaction with incident light, they can transfer energy to neighbouring materials via dipoledipole coupling. When plasmonic nanostructures are coupled with semiconductors, electron-hole pairs are generated below and near the semiconductor band edge, which can then be used in chemical transformations.[[107]] Similarly, PIRET to adsorbed molecules can drive chemical reactions.[[108]] For PIRET to occur, there must be an overlap in the emission spectrum of the plasmonic nanostructures and the absorption spectrum of semiconductors or adsorbed molecules, so this mechanism can work only for certain reactions. 

Optical rectification of the electric field near plasmonic structures has been proposed as the mechanism responsible for detected photocurrents.[[109–112]] Schultz’s group suggested that nanostructured plasmonic materials can generate direct electric fields on the plasmonic surface due to optical rectification of the plasmon resonance, which modulates photoelectrocatalytic activity. To measure the local electric field near Au nanostructures, the authors placed molecules with CN groups in the nanogap between a Au surface and AuNPs and followed the vibrational shifts of the CN bond’s stretch mode, attributed to the Stark effect.[[113]] They then correlated the shifts to the potentials induced by optical rectification by performing separate measurements of the shifts under externally applied potentials. Based on these measurements, the mean calculated surface potential changed by 54 mV under constant illumination at 455 nm with a power density of 540 mWcm[2] .[[109]] 

However, in another case where optical rectification and field enhancements were studied in a plasmonic nanogap under vacuum at 80 K (to ensure the absence of surface photovoltages and thermal current effects), photocurrents due to optical rectification were observed only at extreme laser illumination power densities ( 20 kWcm[2] ), not applicable to the field of plasmonic catalysis.[[112]] The field strengths calculated in these experiments were above 10[8] Vm[1] , consistent with earlier discussed values of 10[7] Vcm[1] required to induce field emission.[[114]] At the same time, the electric field under typical illumination power densities used in PEEC experiments does not exceed hundreds of Vcm[1] .[[6]] 

Similarly, Kos et al. constructed rectifying nanogaps by trapping molecular monolayers between single AuNPs and flat Au surfaces, integrated altogether within a layered electrode structure. Average laser powers of 0.1–0.3 mW were applied with a laser illumination spot diameter of 1.5 μm, translating to kWcm[2] illumination densities and extremely high electric fields of 10[7] –10[8] Vm[1] mW[1/2] .[[111]] These reports imply that alternative factors such as temperature or photovoltage resulting from generated electronhole pairs are more likely to account for the photocurrents observed by different research teams at the much lower illumination power densities typical for PEEC. Thus, while Schultz and colleagues did not observe noticeable broadening of lines and stated that “ _changing temperature is not expected to change the vibrational frequency of CN stretch_ ”, detailed investigation of temperature effects through variable-temperature Raman spectroscopy was not performed in their work and the possibility of electron-hole generation was not considered.[[109]]

Indeed, since it will start and stop much faster than the time resolution limit of electrochemical techniques, it will be difficult to quantifiably differentiate from hot carriers. Thus, Ou et al. measured photocurrents from the ORR on silver nanowire (AgNW) and silver nanosphere (AgNS) electrodes at various wavelengths.[[115]] The effect of plasmoelectric potential in AgNS was found to be negligible. On the other hand, for AgNW, the estimated plasmoelectric potential reached approximately 12 mV ( 0.11 mVW[1] cm[2] ) at 425 nm, while approaching zero at 775 nm at 0.72 V vs Ag2SO4/Ag, which agrees with the observation that the plasmoelectric potential increases as the system moves further away from resonance conditions. However, OCP barely changed under illumination within this range. The authors observed that the photocurrent induced by the plasmoelectric potential depends on the applied bias, with higher photocurrents observed under a bias of 0.72 V than 0.58 V. If the reaction is under mass-transport control at the applied potential, no effect of plasmoelectric potential on the current is expected. Therefore, the reaction must be either under kinetic control or a combination of kinetic and masstransfer control at the potentials where effects of plasmoelectric potential on photocurrent were detected. The linear sweep voltammograms provided by the authors for the ORR in the dark are rather peculiar. There are no diffusioncontrolled peaks, expected on silver electrodes,[[119]] and the current continuously increases with increasing overpotential, suggesting kinetic control of the reaction. However, there was a significant increase in current under O2-saturated conditions compared to measurements in air (‘routine’), indicating mass-transfer control. Moreover, since plasmoelectric potential should not depend on applied potential, only on illumination,[[117]] the relative contribution of plasmoelectric potential to the photocurrent is expected to decrease with increasing overpotential. However, this is contrary to 

the observations made by the authors. This inconsistency implies that either the potential applied to the plasmonic material affects the plasmoelectric potential (which could be investigated by measuring changes of the electrode potential upon illumination at various wavelengths and potential biases in the absence of a catalytic reaction), or that light illumination alters the ORR mechanism. 

## _**4.3. Photovoltage Sustained by the Applied Potential Bias**_ 

Optical rectification and plasmoelectric potentials contribute to photocurrent by generating additional electric potentials on the nanoparticle, independent of the photovoltage originating from hot-carrier generation and separation. Jain’s group have proposed an alternative mechanism of light-induced potential generation where charge build-up occurs when the rates of electron and hole extraction are unequal, resulting in an additional potential on the nanoparticle that can be sustained for longer than the lifetime of the hot-carriers.[[120]] External biases applied to electrodes can facilitate the accumulation of charge on nanoparticles under constant illumination, as positive/negative biases facilitate the extraction of electrons/holes, respectively. The authors reported enhancement of the kinetics of electrochemical reactions through light-induced potentials in various systems, such as ammonia oxidation on Pt-decorated AuNPs,[[49]] the methanol oxidation reaction on Pd-Ag alloy nanotubes[[45]] and the HER on AuNPs.[[121]] 

The supporting citations refer to models or observations from previous reviews and studies which deal with either (a) particle photocatalytic systems in solution, where reactions occur in bulk solutions without electrodes, or (b) conventional photoelectrocatalytic systems constructed with semiconductor materials within the electrodes to both harvest the light and direct the movement of the generated charges. However, the proposed mechanism of potential build-up over continuous illumination due to asymmetric charge transfer is inconsistent with the expected Fermi level equilibration between the electrode and plasmonic nanocatalyst.[[122]] According to conventional electrochemical theory, an applied potential alone cannot selectively extract or de-excite either _only electrons_ or _only holes_ from within a metal conduction band. Rather, the potential applied across the electrochemical cell sets the Fermi level around which the thermal equilibrium energy distribution of charge bearers will fall upon thermalisation. 

No bias towards extraction of only positive or negative charge carriers should occur by an adjacent metal with the same Fermi level, unless either (a) their mobilities differ significantly (hence the typical need for Schottky barriers to limit transport of one type of charge carriers and thereby maintain charge separation), or (b) the (re-)equilibration of the effective electron and hole Fermi levels between the plasmonic nanoparticle and the metal electrode substrate support can be delayed until charge transfer to a reactant occurs (Figure 7A). For example, this could be realised by hindering electron _or_ hole transfer by making tunnelling sufficiently improbable for _one_ of the charge carriers.

## _**5.1. Designing Electrochemical Setups for PEEC**_ 

Properly designed electrochemical cells should be used to investigate the mechanism of current enhancement in PEEC. PEEC measurements are typically performed with a three-electrode setup, with a plasmonic catalyst-loaded electrode serving as the working electrode. Less attention is generally given to the counter and reference electrodes (CE and RE) in PEEC measurements. However, these electrodes can potentially introduce errors into the measurements. Thus, care should be taken when selecting the size and material of the CE. It is generally recommended that the CE be made of material that is inert under the experimental conditions[[123]] and has a significantly larger surface area than the WE. This ensures that the current response of the system is not limited by the CE. This is especially important in PEEC because plasmonic catalysts are nanomaterials with large surface areas. The impedance[[124]] and potential[[125]] of the RE should be carefully checked prior to measurements. The RE should be placed close to the WE, and an electrolyte solution that is sufficiently conductive should be used to reduce the contribution of Ohmic drop (iR drop) to the measured potential.[[126]] In PEEC, accounting for the Ohmic drop is essential to accurately estimate any shift in onset potential under illumination. Detailed information on how to estimate, minimise, or account for the Ohmic drop‘s contribution under specific experimental conditions can be found elsewhere.[[127,128]] Furthermore, it is important to note that the potential of a Ag/AgCl electrode, perhaps the most common RE, is influenced by illumination.[[129]] Therefore, the cell should be designed in such a way that the RE is not exposed to light. 

When conducting PEEC measurements, it is crucial to take into account the absorbance of the electrode support material underneath the plasmonic catalyst, along with the absorbance of the electrolyte and the cell itself. As discussed in Section 3.5., graphitic substrates absorb in a wide visible range. This leads to heating of the supporting material under illumination which contributes to current enhancement due to thermal effects not caused by LSPR. Therefore, transparent supporting materials such as FTO/ITO are preferred for PEEC system studies and development. 

Furthermore, during electrochemical measurements under illumination, it is important to ensure that the entire surface area of the WE is uniformly and consistently illuminated to accurately control the illumination power density and facilitate comparison between different conditions. 

## _**5.2. Stability, Accuracy and Reproducibility**_ 

In PEEC experiments, special attention should be paid to system stability, as illumination can lead to instability, for example due to increases in temperature, which may be erroneously attributed to a catalytic response. Due to the high sensitivity of electrochemical measurements to experimental conditions, it is crucial to ensure that the electrochemical response in PEEC can only be attributed to the 

illumination of the plasmonic material and not influenced by other components of the system, such as the RE or inert electrode support. Control experiments recording baseline responses for comparison should be carried out without the catalytic material but with the reactants and _vice versa_ under experimental conditions identical to those applied to the full photocatalytic system. It is important to ensure that the photocurrents recorded under illumination are reproducible and statistically significantly different from the dark responses. Various tests of statistical significance are available[[130]] and should be chosen to best suit the experimental conditions. 

When comparing continuous-wave illumination with pulsed illumination, continuous-wave methods generally yield lower errors due to the ability to subtract dark Faradaic currents without considering the contribution of capacitive currents, which are more prevalent at shorter times and challenging to account for. Furthermore, if the pulse interval is shorter than the cell time constant (Eqn. 2), accurate current measurements may not be feasible. 

## _**5.3. Experiments to Diagnose the Mechanism of Enhancement**_ 

There are a number of experiments that can be performed to identify the mechanism of current enhancements in PEEC. While it may not always be feasible to conduct all of these experiments, it is essential to perform multiple experiments to credibly demonstrate the significant role of hotcarriers and quantify their contribution. 

Recording _i–t_ curves under alternating (on-off) illumination, as discussed in Section 3.2., is a straightforward experimental approach to reveal the relative proportions of IRC and ISC within the total photocurrent. This approach is particularly useful for assessing the possible contribution of photothermal effects and estimating their percentage contribution. Particularly for the fall, significant curvature over periods of seconds would imply lifetimes of lingering excited states on the order of at least milliseconds, which is inconsistent with most hot-carrier charge separation models if they do not involve barriers/similar systems to prolong the charge separation. 

Hot-carriers can only contribute to photocurrent in conditions where the overall catalytic current is controlled by electron transfer kinetics. Under mass-transport-controlled conditions, any enhancement of current under illumination can be attributed solely to increased substrate transport due to elevated temperatures. Therefore, recording _i–V_ polarization curves or cyclic voltammograms (CVs) both in the dark and under illumination, and comparing the current enhancements at different kinetic regimes (different overpotentials), can help estimate the contribution of temperature. If under experimental conditions a mass-transport-controlled regime can be reached (a mass transport -controlled peak is observed in CV), it can be used to calibrate photothermal contribution (Figure 8). If the enhancement is stronger towards wavelengths somewhat shorter than the LSPR peak, this has been proposed to indicate involvement of hot-carriers from interband transitions. If stronger towards somewhat longer wavelengths, this has been hypothesised to indicate dominant local-field enhancement effects. ## _**6. Summary and Outlook**_ 

Here we have provided an overview of the recent advancements in plasmon-enhanced electrocatalysis, with a focus on the intrinsic mechanisms that may act to improve catalytic activity. The majority of reports on PEEC often overlook or underestimate the effects of temperature in the system, while claiming that hot-carriers are always the main source of observed photocurrents. This misconception arises from the belief that thermal effects only ever make a negligible contribution, given the efficient conduction-based heat dissipation from small nanostructures, even under intense illumination. However, an increasing number of reports demonstrate that _collective_ heating effects _cannot_ be neglected when measurements are performed with an ensemble of nanostructures, which is typically the case in PEEC. Additionally, it has been shown that absorption from underlying electrode supports, such as the graphitic materials commonly used in PEEC photoelectrodes, also leads to heating. Since temperature influences not only substrate mass transport but also the reaction kinetics, even a slight increase in the electrode surface temperature can significantly enhance the current. 

We have discussed several reported approaches for distinguishing between the contributions from different enhancement mechanisms, emphasising the benefits and drawbacks of each method. Recent reports demonstrate particular advantages of electrochemical methods in identifying enhancement mechanisms, as these methods enable direct monitoring of reaction progress by recording the current at the working electrode. While the temporal resolution of conventional electrochemical methods is insufficient to directly detect hot charge carriers, the reported methods for measuring slow and fast current components allow distinguishing between thermal effects and nonthermal effects such as hot-carriers. Furthermore, in addition to the excitation wavelength and illumination power, electrochemical approaches also provide another probing parameter: applied potential bias. This parameter can be varied to change the driving force of the reaction, which can further aid in ascertaining the relative contributions of the various enhancement mechanisms. All this serves to highlight the expanded horizons for investigating the catalysisenhancing mechanisms in PEEC systems versus solutionbased photocatalytic systems, where electrochemical methods can only be used under open circuit conditions. 

Recent reports, however, identify a potential disadvantage of Schottky-free PEEC systems in which a potential bias 

is applied directly to a plasmonic nanostructure or to an underlying conductive electrode support. Namely, these reports bring up the rapid equilibration of Fermi levels between the plasmonic nanomaterial and the biased electrode, which facilitates charge carrier quenching by the electronic circuit. This should be considered in future when designing Schottky-barrier-free PEEC systems. Introducing an insulating layer between the catalyst and the conductive electrode support, or suspending nanoparticles in a solution in a bipolar electrochemistry setup could potentially help to solve issue of fast Fermi level equilibration. 

Overall, more attention should be paid to identifying the mechanisms behind catalytic responses and to designing systems which maximise the contribution of the desired component in order to advance the field. It is important to note that enhanced catalysis due to localised temperature increases is not necessarily inferior to hot-carriers if the plasmonic photosensitiser is particularly efficient at generating photothermal enhancement, and thereby justifies its use. Additionally, when considering the actual relevance and applicability of light-harvesting catalytic systems, factors such as robustness, scalability (economic factors/non-scarcity of materials), need to be considered alongside efficiency.[[131]]
