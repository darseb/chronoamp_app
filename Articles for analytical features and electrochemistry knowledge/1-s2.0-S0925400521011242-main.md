## **1. Introduction** 

Breast cancer is the second most common and deadliest cancer to affect women worldwide [1]. Early-stage cancers are often treatable, but as the cancer stage progresses, the mortality rate increases [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), cancer is the second highest cause of mortality following cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). Many biomarkers have been identified for the early detection of both cancer and CVDs [3,4]. Therefore, the research that aims to detect these biomarkers could help save many lives by enabling early detection and appropriate treatment. Breast cancer is the most common type of cancer 

that is often not detected early enough. It is also very likely to metastasize, which means that cancerous cells leave the original site and spread within the body if not detected early, thereby infecting other organs with cancer. One of the deadliest breast cancer types is human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (Her-2) positive breast cancer. Her-2 is a protein that plays a vital role in the development of breast cancer. A particularly aggressive form of breast cancer can form due to the mutation or overexpression of Her-2. This form of cancer is more aggressive and requires early detection and specialized treatment [5]. Fortunately, Her-2 can be used as a biomarker to detect this type of cancer, which, could improve the patient survival rate when detected early.

Several analytical methods have been used for the detection of Her-2 in tissues and serum. Prominent techniques for the detection of Her-2 are immunohistochemistry (IHC) or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [6]. However, the former has accuracy problems, while the latter is expensive and labor-intensive [6]. As such, sensitive, low-cost, and easy-to-use methods are highly needed to facilitate early detection of Her-2 positive breast cancer. One possible choice is to utilize an electrochemical sensor due to its low-cost and simplicity [7]. In this context, graphene proved to have remarkable electrical and structural properties, including its high electrical and thermal conductivity, excellent mechanical stability and rigidity, high transparency, and large specific surface area [8–11]. Due to its excellent properties, graphene has been incorporated into various electrochemical sensors and biosensors [8,11,12]. Several methods were developed to fabricate and integrate graphene into devices and sensors to make use of its outstanding properties, including chemical vapor deposition (CVD), micromechanical exfoliation, thermal decomposition of silicon carbide, and reduction of graphene oxide [11] [12]. 

Recently, a new non-solution processing technique to fabricate the graphene called laser-scribed graphene (LSG) has become attractive due to its efficiency and low cost [13–15]. The LSG has found many applications in developing electrochemical sensors and biosensors [16–20]. Many advancements have been reported to improve the electrocatalytic activity of LSG electrodes, thus improving the sensitivity by increasing its active surface area. One of the prominent ways to enhance the performances of LSG electrodes is the modification of LSG using highly conducting and catalytic metal nanoparticles such as silver nanowires [21], palladium [22], gold [23], platinum [14,24–27], silver [28,29], and copper [16,30]. Metal oxide nanoparticles such as manganese dioxide [31], hafnium oxide [29], and copper oxide [32,33] have also been utilized to improve the performance of LSG electrodes. Besides, research has been conducted to modify electrodes with 3D nanostructures of palladium [34–37], or gold [38–40] or both [41]. This approach of creating 3D nanostructures has many advantages compared to other alternatives, including its simplicity, precise morphology control, and high performance [34]. The latter is proposed to be due to the increase in electrode surface area, thereby increasing its conductivity. This approach has been explored primarily in nucleic acid detection with gold microelectrodes chips, as it has been found to improve DNA 32 hybridization efficiency [ ] significantly. However, it can be used for plenty of other electrochemical sensing and biosensing applications. Further studies have explored and characterized the phenomenon to obtain more precise manipulation of the nanostructure morphology and surface roughness [34,37]. 3D nanostructured gold was developed following the same procedure as palladium, especially since both are electrodeposited using chloride salts in a diluted solution of hydrochloric acid [38]. To prepare nanostructured gold, an aperture is needed where a conductive area is exposed through an isolation layer. 

The performance of LSG electrodes can be further enhanced by the electrodeposition of 3D gold nanostructures (AuNS) to improve its sensitivity. Recently, we have developed an electrochemical aptasensor based on LSG modified AuNS to detect Her-2 in serum samples [42]. However, the aptamers are fragile, expensive, and require low-temperature storage conditions. In this work, we have redesigned the strategy to develop nanostructured gold enhanced LSG electrodes combined with MIPs to detect Her-2 in serum samples. MIPs are considered as a promising strategy to enhance the selectivity of the electrochemical sensors by using artificial analogous to the natural antigen-antibody systems. MIPs have many advantages such as low-cost, high stability, selectivity, and robustness [43–45]. Thus, all of these factors makes MIPs very promising alternatives to build highly selective sensors [46]. In this context, electropolymerization has found application in the synthesis of MIPs for proteins in aqueous solutions providing several advantages such as the control of the thickness of the polymer film and reducing the MIP synthesis time [47]. In recent years, MIP based electrochemical sensors demonstrated their ability as a promising 

analytical tool for the detection of cancer biomarkers [48]. 

In this context, this is the first report about AuNS modified MIPbased LSG biosensor for the detection of Her-2 samples in serum to the best of our knowledge. This study presents the optimization process of nanostructured gold deposition and PEDOT electropolymerization for MIP including the structural and morphological characterizations by XRD and SEM. Additionally, electrochemical characterization via the electroanalysis techniques such as cyclic voltammetry (CV) and squarewave voltammetry (SWV) are used. Under optimal conditions, the developed sensing strategy was successfully applied to detect Her-2 in serum samples. The LSG-AuNS-MIP sensor was successfully integrated into a custom-made point-of-care (POC) device due to its smaller size, higher stability, and simplicity of production and modification. Thus, the developed strategy will open the door for many other applications of POC devices for different disease biomarkers detection. 

## **2. Experimental** 

## _2.1. Chemicals and reagents_ 

Gold (III) chloride hydrate (HAuCl4), acetic acid, 99 %, 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT) 97 %, sodium dodecyl sulfate, hydrochloric acid and pyrrole 98 %, were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Potassium ferrocyanide and potassium ferricyanide were purchased from MP Biomedicals. Phosphate buffered saline tablets were purchased from Fisher Bioreagents. Her-2 antigen was purchased from Novusbiologicals, USA. Cardiac Troponin-I (cTn-I), cardiac Troponin-T (cTn-T), and cardiac Troponin-C (cTn-C) were purchased from abcam. Cholesterol, dopamine, myoglobin, and glucose were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. All chemicals were used as received without any further treatment. 

## _2.2. Solutions_ 

HAuCl4 was dissolved in 0.1 M HCl to yield a final concentration of 0.05 M for HAuCl4, which is then diluted as needed. Her-2 antigen was diluted in a 0.01 M PBS to prepare the desired concentrations. For electrochemical detection, a solution of 2.5 mM potassium ferrocyanide and 2.5 mM potassium ferricyanide was prepared in 0.1 M PBS containing 0.1 M KCl and used as a redox probe. EDOT solution (10 mM) was prepared in glass containers and stored at 4[◦] C in the dark. All solutions were prepared in de-ionized (DI) water. All measurements were performed at laboratory temperature. 

## _2.3. Fabrication of laser-scribed graphene electrodes_ 

LSG electrodes were fabricated by irradiating a polyimide sheet with a CO2 laser at an optimized laser power, vertical distance, and exposure duration. The commercially available polyimide (PI) sheet purchased from Utech Products (USA) with a width of 12 in. was used as the substrate to scribe the graphene. The CO2 laser, with a wavelength of 10.6 μm and spot size of ~150 μm was purchased from Universal Laser Systems® PLS6.75. The laser writing speed and power have been characterized and optimized in our previous work [49]. The electrodes were then modified by passivating the working, reference, and counter electrodes by applying nail polish in a precise manner to isolate the electrode areas.

All electrochemical processes and characterizations were carried out using Multi-sens with the accompanying multi-trace software from PalmSens company BV Houten (Netherlands). The setup can accommodate up to four sensors at the same time enabling better throughput analysis. To study the flexibility and reliability of the developed LSG-AuNSMIP sensor and its effect on the analytical response, the LSG, LSGAuNS, and LSG-AuNS-MIP electrodes were bent at different angles (45 ◦, 90 ◦, and 180 ◦) for 5 min. A significant decrease in the current response was observed by applying 180[◦] bending for different time intervals (Fig. 3c). The values 

of response decrease were 18 %, 14 %, and 10 % for LSG, LSG-AuNS, and LSG-AuNS-MIP, respectively. 

Another test of these electrodes’ flexibility and reliability was performed by twisting the electrodes over time (Fig. 3d). The obtained data showed that even after 120 s of twisting, the electrodes keep a high current response. The LSG-AuNS platform and LSG-AuNS-MIP showed excellent results in terms of flexibility and thus demonstrated the reliability of these platform to build electrochemical sensors with a longtime stability. These results confirmed the high stability and flexibility of the developed LSG-AuNS-MIP sensor, making it a suitable candidate that can support rough conditions at the industrial level or on the applications on wearable devices that require such flexible conditions.

## _3.4. Optimization of electropolymerization experimental conditions_ 

## _3.4.1. Selection of the functional monomer_ 

To optimize the MIP sensor, the first step is the selection of a suitable conducting polymer. Indeed, three conducting polymers have been tested using the LSG-AuNS sensing platform, which are polypyrrole (PPy), polyaminothiophenol (PATP), and poly (3, 4- ethylenedioxythiophene) 

(PEDOT). The first polymer tested on top of the AuNS was PPy (Fig. 5a). The deposition approach was utilized with cyclic voltammetry, and the parameters were 20 deposition cycles, and the voltage was swept from – 0.7 V to 0.5 V in 200 mM pyrrole in 50 mM PBS. The resultant LSG-AuNS-PPy was tested in 2.5 mM of ferri/ferrocyanide solution, and the results were compared with LSG-AuNS. After deposition of the PPy onto the LSG-AuNS, the increase in charge transfer resistance might be due to the excessive layers deposited onto the electrode surface. There is no synergistic effect of the AuNS and PPy nanocomposite. Hence, we have tested other conducting polymers onto the LSG-AuNS electrode. The second polymer experimented was aminothiophenol (PATP), which was proposed to have a more favorable interaction with the underlying AuNS layer due to the presence of thiol groups [52]. Upon the polymerization of ATP on AuNS, the peak current response was hindered greatly (Fig. 5b). The polymerization was carried out via CV with potential from – 0.3 V to 0.7 V for 10 cycles. The monomer concentration was 10 mM of ATP in 50 mM of PBS, and the scan rate was 50 mV/s. However, due to its poor MIP performance, other options were considered. The third monomer tested for the electropolymerization on top of the LSG-AuNS electrode was EDOT. The results showed a good synergistic combination between AuNS and PEDOT film 

successfully formed on the top of the working electrode (Fig. 5c). Hence, further optimization was performed to enhance the electrochemical response of the LSG-AuNS-PEDOT. 

After selecting PEDOT as the suitable polymer to prepare the MIP film onto the LSG-AuNS working electrodes, it is crucial to optimize the PEDOT film deposition parameters. Indeed, several parameters such as the applied potential, the concentration of EDOT, and the electropolymerization time were optimized. In this section, the parameters that yield the highest current response were chosen to fabricate the MIP sensor. The applied potential during the electropolymerization of EDOT was explored from 0.70 V to 0.90 V, where 0.85 V yielded the highest current intensity response (Fig. S3a). The monomer concentration (EDOT) was explored from 10 mM to 40 mM, where 10 mM was found to have the most negligible capacitance current, and thus it was chosen for further experiments (Fig. S3b). Finally, the polymerization time was explored between 70 s, 120 s, and 180 s where the former yielded the highest current intensity (Fig. S3c). Hence, the most optimal PEDOT electropolymerization parameters on the LSG-AuNS working electrode are 0.85 V in 10 mM EDOT for 70 s. 

## _3.4.2. Optimization of the MIP film preparation_

The first step is the adsorption step, where Her-2 is incorporated into the polymer matrix. The second step is the template extraction, where Her-2 is extracted from the polymer matrix to create the selective cavities that will be later used to capture the Her-2 target analyte. The third step is the rebinding, where Her-2 is reintroduced again to the sensor for detection.

A high concentration of target analyte (Her-2) adsorbed on LSG-AuNS might cause the aggregation leading to a decrease in the number of specific cavities and the sensitivity of the sensor. ## _3.5. Sensitivity of the LSG-AuNS-MIP sensor_ 

After optimizing the experimental parameters, we investigated the electrochemical response of the LSG-AuNS-MIP sensor towards the detection of different concentrations of Her-2. The concentration range tested was 1–200 ng/mL. The selection of the concentration range was in agreement with the positive and negative Her-2 level values in the breast cancer patients and healthy samples [55]. As expected, as much as we increase the concentration of Her-2, the response of the LSG-AuNS-MIP decreases in the solution of the redox probe of [Fe(CN)6][3][−][/4][−] . This current decrease is due to the binding of Her-2 by the imprinted cavities of the LSG-AuNS-MIP (Fig. 6a). 

Fig. 6b shows the corresponding calibration curve obtained with the LSG-AuNS-MIP incubated in different concentrations of Her-2. In detail, a logarithmic, linear relationship was fitted for the electrochemical responses obtained for the Her-2 captured by the LSG-AuNS-MIP following the equation Δ _I_ ox = 48.04 lg [Her-2] + 24.96, _R_[2 ] = 0.992. As a result, the developed sensor exhibited a LOD of 0.43 ng/mL for Her-2 detection. These results confirmed that gold nanostructured LSG electrodes could be excellent candidates in sensing these types of biomarkers thanks to their easiness of preparation, practicality combined with high selectivity and sensitivity of the MIP technology. As can be seen in Table S1, different Her-2 biosensing approaches have been reported. The reported studies demonstrated the ability to detect Her-2 in real samples below the cut-off concentration value (15 ng/mL), confirming their useful clinical diagnosis application. Our developed strategy based on LSGAuNS-MIP sensing confirmed its capability successfully to compete with other bioassay sensing systems to detect Her-2. The MIP-based sensor proved its low-cost, high sensitivity, and selectivity towards the detection of Her-2.
