The high surface area and favorable electrocatalytic Direct-laser writing properties also enable the design of small electrochemical devices while retaining the desired electrochemical Voltammetry performance. In this work, we systematically investigated the effect of LIG working electrode size, from 0.8 mm Carbon nanomaterials Electrochemical sensors to 4.0 mm diameter, on their electrochemical properties, since it has been widely assumed that the electrochemistry of LIG electrodes is independent of size above the microelectrode size regime. The background and faradaic current from cyclic voltammetry (CV) of an outer-sphere redox probe [Ru(NH3)6][3][+] showed that smaller LIG electrodes had a higher electrode roughness factor and electroactive surface ratio than those of the larger electrodes. Moreover, CV of the surface-sensitive redox probes [Fe(CN)6][3][– ] and dopamine revealed that smaller electrodes exhibited better electrocatalytic properties, with enhanced electron transfer kinetics. Scanning electron microscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy showed that the physical and chemical surface structure were different at the electrode center versus the edges, so the electrochemical properties of the smaller electrodes were improved by having rougher surface, more density of the graphitic edge planes, and more oxide-containing groups. The difference could be explained by the different photothermal reaction time from the laser scribing process that causes different stable carbon morphology to form on the polymer surface. Our results give a new insight on relationships between surface structure and electrochemistry of LIG electrodes and are useful for designing miniaturized electrochemical devices. 

## **1. Introduction** 

Graphene, the two-dimensional carbon nanomaterial with a single planar sheet of sp[2 ] carbon atoms, has become popular as a carbon nanomaterial to fabricate electrochemical devices due to its superior physical and chemical properties [1–3]. Previous strategies to prepare graphene-based materials and devices include chemical vapor deposition [4], coating its dispersion on a conductive material [5,6], and screen-printing graphene-based ink [7]. Recently, direct lasing on a polymer substrate was found to produce a graphene layer, termed laser-induced graphene (LIG), as first reported by the Tour group [8]. LIG was produced by CO2 infrared laser scribing on a polyimide (PI) 

sheet to produce porous graphene [8,9]. This photothermal process involves transferring the laser energy to the PI, which is an aromatic carbon polymer, followed by covalent bond breaking, releasing of gaseous products, and rearrangement of carbon atoms to form porous LIG [10]. The wide availability of laser cutters and control software also makes it easier to design a patterned area to produce LIG. Thus, fabrication of LIG is simple, scalable, and reagentless, all of which are advantageous over other electrode fabrication strategies. 

With its high specific surface area, electron mobility, and mechanical strength [11], LIG has been emerging as a novel electrode material for smart devices such as electrochemical sensors, batteries, and supercapacitors due to its simple fabrication and superior electrochemistry [9,

12,13]. For analytical chemistry, LIG electrodes have been produced for _in vitro_ neurotransmitter and small molecules detection [14,15], glucose sensing [16,17], COVID-19 electrochemical diagnostics [18,19], and other electroanalytical applications [20–23]. Currently, many studies have investigated the effects of laser parameters on electrochemical properties of LIG electrodes. Laser exposure time was found to affect the surface area and electron transfer kinetics toward [Fe(CN)6][3][–][/4][– ] probe and 4-aminophenol electrode reaction [24]. IR and UV irradiation in LIG electrode fabrication for dopamine detection was also compared on their sensitivities and miniaturization abilities [25]. Fundamental studies have been also performed on the LIG electrodes to explain their superior electrochemistry. For instance, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy of LIG electrodes exhibited their high porosities, but nonhomogeneous, with non-ideal capacitance, which increased after longer electrolyte exposure [26]. Enhanced defects and edge planes with extended porous network were found to accelerate the electron transfer kinetics of LIG electrodes toward many redox probes with diffusion-controlled process [27]. Moreover, hydrophobicity of the LIG electrode also provided stability for potentiometric measurements of ions by preventing the change in physical interface between the selective membrane and transducer [28]. These works demonstrated that LIG enhanced electrode surface area and electron transfer kinetics, leading to better peak separation, higher sensitivity, and/or better limits of detection for quantitative analysis. 

Despite their widespread use, all prior work has assumed no differences in electrochemistry based on size above the microelectrode domain. Until now, there have been no studies that evaluated the impact of electrode size on electrochemical performance. Many emerging applications require the fabrication of small electrodes and new geometries for modern sensing technologies such as in wearable electronics and _in vivo_ analysis [29–31]. Systematic electrochemical studies of the miniaturized electrodes are important, since electrode size and shape might affect the electrochemical properties of the electrode due to its surface micro/nanostructure and confined mass transfer [32–34], in addition to the surface area and chemical components. In this work, we explored the effects of the working electrode diameter (“size”) of LIG electrodes on their electrochemistry for the first time. Here, cyclic voltammetry (CV) of supporting electrolyte, outer-sphere redox probe, and surface-sensitive redox probe was utilized to investigate the electrochemical properties of LIG with different electrode size. Physicochemical characterization of the LIG electrode surfaces was then performed to understand the performance in terms of electrode geometry, roughness factor, and chemical structure, all of which are important to understand their electrochemistry [33–35]. This study highlights the importance of LIG electrode size and surface properties on electrochemical properties, which is essential for designing small electrochemical devices for modern applications. 

## **2. Material and methods** 

## _2.1. Chemicals_ 

Polyimide (PI) sheet, with 125-µm thickness, was purchased from Victor & R (Bangkok, Thailand). Potassium ferricyanide (K3[Fe(CN)6]), hexaammineruthenium(III) chloride ([Ru(NH3)6]Cl3), dopamine hydrochloride, potassium chloride, sodium phosphate monobasic monohydrate, and 95 % ethanol (AR grade) were purchased from SigmaAldrich. All aqueous solutions were prepared in ultrapure water with 18.2 MΩ cm resistivity or higher (MilliQ). 

## _2.2. LIG electrodes fabrication_ 

The LIG three-electrode systems with connectors were fabricated using 10.6 μm CO2 laser cutter (Flux Beamo) with 200-µm spot diameter. The working electrode diameter (0.8 mm to 4.0 mm), position, and pattern for direct laser writing was designed in Adobe Illustrator. The 

size of the counter electrode was also increased proportionally to the working electrode to prevent the limitation of the current flow, evidenced by unchanged peak current and potential from further enlarging the counter electrode. The PI sheet was attached to a plastic sheet and cleaned with 95 % ethanol, then it was placed on the laser cutter platform. Then, direct laser writing was performed according to the designed file under the ambient condition with 6.9 W laser power and 100 mm/s laser speed. The process was performed twice on the same area to ensure the electrical connection between the parts. From our equipment, we found that laser scribing twice improved the electrochemical properties and reproducibility of the obtained LIG electrodes, and laser speed affected the integrity and size of the electrode. Note that the optimal condition might be varied between different equipment. Lastly, to define the working electrode area, a strip of insulating transparent tape was applied to cover the remaining graphene below the circular portion with pressure to prevent leaking and ensure the LIG exposed area. 

## _2.3. Electrode surface characterization_ 

Physical characterization of the fabricated LIG electrodes was conducted through optical microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Raman spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Optical images of the electrodes were taken by a smartphone via Chula Smart Lens (Chulalongkorn University, Thailand) at 40X magnification. SEM micrographs of the gold-sputtered samples were obtained using a scanning electron microscope (JEOL, JSM-IT500HR, Japan) operated within 10 kV in the vacuum mode. Raman spectra were acquired using a dispersive micro-Raman spectrometer (DXR, Thermo Scientific, USA) using a 532-nm excitation laser. XPS spectra were obtained at Axis Ultra DLD (Kratos Analytical, UK) using the Al Kα monochromatic X-ray source (1486.6 eV) with the pass energy of 40 eV for electronic state analysis. 

## _2.4. Electrochemical measurement and calculation_ 

CV was performed using a potentiostat (EmStat4S, PalmSens, Netherlands) controlled with the PSTrace software. All measurements were conducted using the fabricated all-LIG electrode (working, auxiliary, and reference electrodes were LIG). A 50-µL test solution was dropped to cover all three electrodes, and the CV experiments were scanned vs a LIG pseudo-reference electrode. To compare the properties of LIG electrodes, electrochemical measurements were also performed on the 3.0-mm diameter screen-printed graphene electrode (SPGE), inhouse prepared [36].

## **3. Results and discussion** 

In this work, a LIG three-electrode platform was fabricated by laser scribing all conducting parts (working electrode, reference electrode, auxiliary electrode, and connectors) on a PI sheet, to investigate the sizedependent properties of the electrode. Fig. 1 shows photographs of LIG electrodes with different working electrode diameters varying between 0.8 and 4.0 mm. The photograph exhibits the visual change of the PI sheet from yellow to porous black layer, the typical feature of LIG from PI sheet [8]. The magnified images reveal that the LIG electrodes are not smooth, especially at the edge, and some areas might receive less photothermal energy from the laser because of the relatively large laser spot size (0.2 mm), which is an instrumental limitation. 

LIG electrodes have been demonstrated for electrochemical sensing applications, but a better understanding of its surface and electrochemical properties will be informative for further electrode design and optimization. Therefore, the size of the LIG working electrode was varied to analyze the size-dependent electrochemical properties. A constant proportionality between the faradaic current and the electrode geometrical area, with the same electron transfer kinetics and reversibility behavior, has been assumed for all previous LIG electrode studies. However, the electrochemical characterization using common redox 

probes [Ru(NH3)6][3][+] , [Fe(CN)6][3][–] , and dopamine, showed a strong sizedependent trend. Further surface characterization by SEM, Raman spectroscopy, and XPS at the electrode center and edge also revealed significant size-dependent properties of the LIG electrodes. 

## _3.1. Size-dependent surface roughness factor and electroactive surface area of the LIG electrode_ 

CV of a background electrolyte 1 M KCl was performed at each LIG electrode (Fig. 2A) using a screen-printed graphene electrode (SPGE) as a comparison to analyze double-layer capacitance ( _C_ d). Cyclic voltammograms show an increased background charging current as the electrode diameter increases. Graphene could generally enhance the electrode surface area and background charging current proportionally [32], so the _C_ d, normalized to electrode surface area should be constant. However, Fig. 2B shows the computed _C_ d in µF/cm[2 ] of LIG electrodes as a function of size, and their electrode roughness factor, calculated against the _C_ d of GCE, which has been treated as a flat electrode [35,39, 40], is shown in Table S1. The smallest LIG electrode (0.8 mm) had the highest roughness factor, and the roughness decreased with increasing electrode diameter to the largest LIG electrode (4.0 mm). This trend was significant ( _p <_ 0.05, one-way ANOVA), which was unanticipated because the electrode material should be the same regardless of its size at these size scales. However, the results suggest there is a size dependent micro/nanostructure from the laser scribing of the LIG electrode. 

Next, the electroactive surface area of each LIG electrode was analyzed using CV of an outer sphere redox probe 1 mM [Ru(NH3)6][3][+] in ˇ 1 M KCl with the modified Randles–Sev[ˇ] cík equation. Fig. 2C shows the increased anodic and cathodic peak current with the increased electrode size, similar to that of background charging current. However, the current did not change proportionally to the electrode geometrical surface area, another evidence showing that LIG electrodes have size dependent electrochemical properties. In other words, if all LIG had the same properties, then the peak current from 0.8-mm electrode should be 1/25 of that from 4.0-mm electrode, but the result shows significantly higher peak current from the 0.8-mm electrode than expected, about 1/8 of that of the 4.0-mm electrode. Therefore, the electroactive area was calculated by varying the CV scan rate (Fig. S1) and dividing the measured area by the geometric area to give an electroactive area percentage. Fig. 2D reveals that the electroactive area ratio of the 0.8-mm LIG electrode was the highest among all LIG electrodes and then decreased with the increasing LIG diameter, down to the 4.0-mm LIG electrode. This trend was significant ( _p <_ 0.0001, one-way ANOVA) and warrants further investigation. 

## _3.2. Size-dependent electron transfer kinetics and reversibility of LIG electrode_ 

The electron transfer kinetics of the LIG electrodes was explored using CV of [Fe(CN)6][3][–] , a surface-sensitive redox probe. Fig. 3A shows the cyclic voltammograms of 1 mM [Fe(CN)6][3][– ] in 1 M KCl at 0.1 V/s scan rate. The anodic peak current increased with the LIG electrode size, demonstrating the increasing electrode surface area, but the current density, based on the geometrical surface area, of smaller LIG electrodes was still higher, similar to that of [Ru(NH3)6][3][+] . The voltammograms also exhibited a quasi-reversible behavior from the equal anodic and cathodic peak currents with the Δ _E_ p _>_ 59 mV. Both plots show that all LIG electrode exhibited smaller Δ _E_ p than that of SPGE, indicating better electrocatalytic performance since three-dimensional structure of LIG leads to better accessibility of the electroactive species and further enhances the redox reaction [43].

The effect of scan rate on the CV of dopamine was also investigated. These different properties could be rationalized by size-dependent physical and surface structural properties of LIG electrodes, described in the next section.

## _3.3. Size-dependent physical and chemical surface structure of LIG electrodes_ 

To analyze the physical surface micro/nanostructure of the fabricated LIG electrodes as a function of size, SEM images (Fig. 5) were obtained at different magnifications and locations. Fig. 5A and 5E show images of the 0.8 and 4.0-mm LIG electrode, respectively. Both electrode surfaces had rough and irregular surfaces in the area scribed by the CO2 laser compared to the smooth surface of the unscribed PI sheet. In comparing electrode surfaces, the surface of the larger LIG electrodes was more ordered and smoother than the smaller LIG electrode. SEM images were also taken at higher magnifications at the edge (Fig. 5B and 5F) and center (Fig. 5C and 5G) of the electrodes, illustrating the porous and interconnected fiber-liked structure of the LIG electrode that is believed to be a result from the rapid release of the gaseous product from the photothermal reaction [8,9]. At the electrode edge of the 0.8-mm LIG electrode (Fig. 5B), the SEM image revealed larger pores in the 

structure than those in the 4.0-mm LIG electrode (Fig. 5F), which could result in the differences in the electron transfer and accessibility of the electroactive species to the electrode surface [33,48]. On the other hand, the electrode centers for both electrodes had a similar microporous structure (Fig. 5C and 5G). The cross-sectional images of both electrodes (Fig. 5D and 5H) also show the depth of the formed graphene to be about 70 µm. This LIG porous morphology at this scale could provide enhanced electrode surface area and might exhibit the confined electrochemical behavior of and electrocatalytic properties toward the redox probes.

These physical and surface chemical properties dictated the electrochemical properties of the LIG electrodes with different sizes.

Redox reactions at smaller LIG electrodes were controlled by surface confinement, differing from the larger electrodes, which were purely diffusion-controlled (Section 3.2). These electrochemical properties were then linked to their physical morphology and chemical structure on the electrode surface. SEM images show that smaller LIG electrode had larger pore size and less ordered microstructure while having more edge planes and oxygen-containing carbon functional groups than the larger electrodes, revealed by Raman and XPS spectroscopy (Section 3.3). 

Carbon electrode materials are diverse, and their surface physical and chemical structures are known to affect their electrochemical performance [39]. From our results, smaller LIG electrodes had larger pore structure and higher roughness factor than the larger electrodes. This morphology difference causes the smaller electrode to have higher reversibility, similar to carbon nanopipette electrode [48]. Extended porous structure and high roughness factor also provided more accessible areas for the species to undergo electron transfer, enhancing electroactive area ratio. In addition, more amount of carbon edge planes and oxygen-containing functional groups in the LIG electrode surface structure enhanced the electron transfer kinetics at the smaller electrode due to the enhanced interaction, especially for surface-sensitive or inner sphere redox probes. Many studies have shown that edge plane exhibited better electrocatalytic properties than the basal planes [39,51], which explained the difference between small and large LIG electrodes. Physical and surface chemical structures are therefore important to explain the size-dependent electrochemistry of LIG electrodes. 

Why did the LIG electrodes with different sizes have different physical and chemical surface structures? Consider the direct laser writing in LIG production, which involves a photothermal reaction converting laser energy to atomize the components in PI sheet, release gaseous products, and rearrange the carbon atoms to form hexagonal lattice of graphene [9]. The amount of energy controlled by laser parameters is important to determine the carbon forms. In practice, the laser beam, with a specific resolution, scanned through the sample with some overlapping between each beam, therefore the center area of LIG accepted the lasing energy from the laser spot many times, while the edge or border area of LIG may be scribed only once or twice. This difference in the accepted energy was probable to induce the different physical and chemical surface structure between the center and the edge of the LIG electrode to be in the different final forms, yielding different electrochemical properties. The LIG electrode edge which could probably accept less lasing energy might not completely convert to pure graphene but left some oxygen-containing functional group and porous structure that apparently enhanced the electron transfer kinetics of the LIG electrode. This variation was amplified when the LIG electrode was smaller because the edge area had more proportion to the overall electrode area, leading to the observed better electrochemical properties of the small LIG electrodes. Future studies should utilize microelectrochemical techniques such as scanning electrochemical microscopy, coupled with computational tools, to investigate and model the local electrochemical properties in the LIG electrodes. 

## **4. Conclusions** 

The effect of LIG electrode size on its electrochemistry was explored in this work by CV, SEM, Raman spectroscopy, and XPS to correlate the electrochemical properties with its physical and chemical surface structure. Smaller LIG electrodes (down to 0.8-mm diameter) contained rougher surface, more proportion of edge planes to basal planes, more oxygen-containing functional groups at the electrode surface than those of larger LIG electrodes (up to 4.0-mm diameter). These differences led to the enhanced electroactive area ratio, electron transfer kinetics, and reversibility of the smaller electrodes. The process controlling electron transfer at the smaller LIG electrode was surface confinement, while that at the larger electrode was pure diffusion. Future research can explore the design and fabrication of smaller LIG electrodes in novel electrochemical devices to yield better desired properties while considering their size-dependent electrochemical properties.
