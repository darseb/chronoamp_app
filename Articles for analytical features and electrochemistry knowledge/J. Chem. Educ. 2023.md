ABSTRACT: Many aspects of electrochemical education, such as electroanalysis demonstrations, require three-electrode-cell experimental setups. Many researchers and educators use screen-printed electrochemical electrodes, which are miniature and (2 disposable but are relatively expensive and use proprietary fabrication components. The present article describes how to laser-scribe complete three-electrode grapheneHey: based electrodes that can compete with (and, in some regards, even rival) commercial screen-printed electrodes. The process of optimization of the lasing procedure to produce laser-induced graphene using almost any laser cutter available to the reader is described. A way to form a Ag/AgCl pseudoreference electrode on the graphene trace i 3 * voltammetry to complete the three-electrode cell, without usage of commercial inks or complicated | om e fabrication | printing technologies, is demonstrated. Electrochemical characterization of the ee SS | electrode surface, reference electrode stability, and student practice demonstration of analytic applications of the produced cells (determination of dopamine concentration and coffee polyphenols) are described. These experiments and procedures act both as a method of fabrication of laser-scribed electrodes and as an educational material for introduction to electrodeposition, voltammetry, and electroanalysis.

## ■ **[INTRODUCTION]** 

The advent of cheap electronics prototyping and 3d-printing technology brought on solutions to replace expensive electrochemical equipment with lab-made analogues. Numerous “doit-yourself” (DIY) instructions for the production of potentiostats devices crucial to implementation of any electrochemical methods have been reported.[1][−][4] While these devices can almost never compete with research-grade brethren in operational parameters, they make electrochemical science and education more accessible.[3] 

Electrodes are vital to any electrochemical setup. The nature and function of various electrodes and their roles in electrochemical cells are covered in textbooks and practical guides.[5][−][7] While some electrodes, such as stainless steel or even pencil lead graphite,[8] are easy to make and utilize, some others are expensive and require complex fabrication. For this reason, production of low-cost electrodes for research and educational use remains a major field of research.[9][,][10] Recent advances in 3d-printing have allowed for rapid and cheap production of electrodes,[9][,][11][,][12] including reference electrodes, which are extremely important in voltammetric and potentiometric measurements. Reference electrodes, as the name implies, are used as a reference point, against which the potential of other electrodes is measured in an electrochemical cell.[5] This important role calls for high stability and reproducibility of the electrode, and thus affects the pricing, with the average commercial reference electrode costing in the 

range of $100−300 USD. Such a price for a brittle piece of equipment that requires high maintenance and is prone to permanent damage hinders its employment in educational laboratories. Fortunately, modern advances provide plenty of lab-made solutions for reference electrodes as well, including robust hydrogen electrodes made of micropipette tips[13] and adaptable 3d-printed,[9] flexible inkjet-printed,[14] and even fully paper-based[15] reference electrodes. Employment of these DIY electrodes not only can be cost-saving, but the fabrication protocols themselves can be used as educational tools, introducing students to basic operational concepts of electrodes and the technologies involved in their production. 

On the other hand, some modern electrochemical systems are hard to make in-house. For example, screen-printed electrodes (SPEs), which are miniature three-electrode electrochemical cells, are made by application of multiple layers of various conductive and insulating inks onto flat ceramic or plastic substrates in a complex pattern. These devices are extremely popular in electroanalytic research[16][,][17] and in educational

Thus, the task of the present work was to develop an analogue to SPEs that could provide comparable or better performance in terms of electroanalytical applications and be built “from the ground up” with the students. 

In this regard, laser-induced graphene (LIG) technology is promising. The LIG process, serendipitously invented by Lin et al. in 2014,[21] involves irradiating precursor materials with a laser, transferring enough energy to carbonize the surface in such a way to form graphene. While it has been shown that many materials can be employed in this process,[22] polyimide (Kapton) remains the most popular, owing to its low price, high accessibility, and flexibility. Commercial CO2 infrared laser cutters/engravers, commonly found in university machine shops, fiber lasers,[23] and even low power diode lasers[24][,][25] can be used for LIG production. Highly conductive LIG traces are now studied for electrochemical applications sensors, supercapacitors, and other devices which are well covered by a number of reviews.[23][,][26][−][28] 

With the aim to introduce LIG into electrochemical education settings, the present article describes the production of complete LIG-based cells, functionally identical to a standard SPE with three electrodes including a Ag/AgCl pseudoreference electrode. The described procedures are helpful educational tools that were used to introduce students to the nanomaterial fabrication and electrode concepts, electrodeposition, voltammetry, electrochemical redox reactions, and electroanalysis. The use of nanomaterial-based miniature cells instead of conventional electrochemical setups also introduces students to current trends in electroanalytical chemistry, such as biomedical applications in point-of-care devices.[29][−][31]

## **Reference Electrode Formation** 

In order to form a complete three-electrode cell, a reference electrode is also needed. Usually, silver inks are used for this purpose.[34] There are also some examples of successful employment of silver electrodeposition on graphene to produce pseudoRE;[11][,][28] they use commercial electroplating solutions and devices, which may hinder the potential learning experience and reproducibility. 

Herein, in order to produce a Ag/AgCl pseudoRE for the three-electrode cells, electrochemical reduction of a silver ammonia complex Ag(NH3)2OH to produce a metallic silver coating on one of the LIG electrodes was utilized (as depicted in Figure 1). The ammonia−silver complex is more stable than silver nitrate in aqueous solutions, leading to more even and stable coatings as compared to electrodeposition of pure AgNO3.[35][,][36] An illustrated and detailed description of the process of pseudoRE formation is provided in the Supporting Information. 

Briefly, in two-electrode mode, a voltage of −3 V is applied to the electrode designed as RE in a cell with Ag(NH3)2OH solution. Then, a AgCl layer is produced by the oxidation of silver in the cell filled with diluted HCl at +1 V voltage. The photographs in Figure 1 show a typical three-electrode cell in each stage of fabrication. 

This process is quite cheap, does not use commercial silverplating solutions that can contain cyanide, does not require stencils or other precautions to preserve the geometry of the electrode, and acts as an excellent material for students’ introduction to electroplating.

## ■ **[LIG ELECTRODES IN ELECTROANALYSIS]** 

A series of simple student experiments that show how nanomaterials can be used to tremendously improve the electrochemical sensing performance is proposed on examples of dopamine detection and beverage analysis. Point-by-point instructions for both are provided in the Supporting Information. 

Dopamine (DA) is one of the most important analytes for biomedical sensor development.[40][,][41] DA is a neuromodulator molecule that plays a crucial role in the human nervous system, and its concentration in bodily fluids is a biomarker for a number of medical conditions. DA is also a redox-active compound, which makes its detection via electrochemical methods particularly attractive.

To gauge the suitability of the fabrication of LIG electrodes and their electroanalytical use in educational settings, a practical lab was carried out and a survey was conducted with ten postgraduate students (most majoring in electrical and/or biomedical engineering). Participation was voluntary, and the students were ensured confidentiality. 

The students have completed the “Laser-induced graphene electrodes” lab practice, where they have used a laser engraver to produce several LIG traces on polyimide tape, used electrodeposition to form Ag/AgCl pseudoRE, and employed the cells fabricated by them in electroanalysis. The practice is described in the Supporting Information and in the student handout. 

The main goal of the practice was to introduce students to nanomaterial fabrication on the example of laser-induced graphene, how this material can be used in electrochemical cells, how conductive materials can be coated via electroplating, and, ultimately, how all of this knowledge helps in modern electroanalytical problems. ## ■ **[CONCLUSIONS]** 

In conclusion, it is shown that laser-induced graphene electrodes that can be easily produced using laser cutters available to many university workshops can be an excellent alternative to screenprinted electrodes in electrochemical education and research. Optimization of the LIG formation, fabrication of threeelectrode cells using the material, and demonstration of its outstanding properties in electroanalysis are all excellent lab practices. The three-electrode cells produced in this manner are cheap (see Supporting Information Table S2), can have any desirable geometry, and demonstrate good electrochemical performance. The procedures described herein introduce students to nanomaterials, fabrication techniques, and electrochemical measurements for material characterization and analytical applications (dopamine detection and coffee analysis). The student educational practice lab has shown that the LIG-based cell fabrication technique described here, as well as employment of the produced miniature devices for electroanalysis, is an exciting learning experience in electrochemistry, electroanalysis, and sensors.
