## **The Nicholson Method of Determination of the Standard Rate Constant of a Quasireversible Redox Couple Employing Cyclic Voltammetry: Everything One Needs to Know!**

ABSTRACT: For more than 50 years, the Nicholson method has been the predominant technique for the electrochemical kinetic analysis of quasireversible redox couples, primarily due to its simplicity in determining the standard rate constant ( _k_[0] ). This method necessitates only the peak potential separation (Δ _E_ p) to derive the kinetic parameter, _ψ_ , where the slope of the _ψ_ versus ( _nπD_ o _Fν_ / _RT_ )[−][1/2] plot directly reflects _k_[0] . There are two approaches to ascertain _ψ_ : one can either use the _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p dataset or an empirical equation. Nonetheless, the existence of three distinct _ψ_ vs Δ _E_ p datasets and 12 empirical equations in the literature creates ambiguity regarding the optimal choice for kinetic analysis. Furthermore, there is a lack of comprehensive guidelines 

and best practices regarding the correct way of application of the Nicholson approach. Researchers often overlook the inherent limitations and drawbacks associated with the Nicholson method, ultimately resulting in a considerable volume of misinterpreted findings in the existing literature. This Perspective examines the theoretical foundations and constraints of Nicholson’s approach, assesses the validity of different proposed _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p datasets and empirical equations, and tackles several fundamental questions regarding the Nicholson approach. Moreover, this approach offers a fresh _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p dataset for each mV interval of Δ _E_ p, thus obviating the need for empirical equations to determine _ψ_ for a particular Δ _E_ p value. The claims are further corroborated by the experimental evaluation of _k_[0] for [Ru(NH3)6][3+] /[Ru(NH3)6][2+] , [Fe(CN)6][3][−] /[Fe(CN)6][4][−] , and Eu[3+] /Eu[2+] redox couples. 

## ■ **[INTRODUCTION]** 

Redox couples can be categorized into three types: reversible, quasireversible, and irreversible. The interplay between electron transfer kinetics ( _k_[0] ) and mass transfer kinetics, represented by the mass transport coefficient ( _m_ T) is crucial for determining the reversibility of a redox couple. A redox couple is considered reversible when _k_[0] significantly surpasses _m_ T, whereas it is classified as irreversible when _k_[0] is considerably lower than mT. When _k_[0] and _m_ T are comparable, the couple is classified as quasireversible.[1] The characteristics of quasireversible redox couples place them in an intermediary position between reversible and irreversible couples. Unlike their reversible and irreversible counterparts, quasireversible couples lack a direct analytical expression for kinetic analysis.[1] As a result, researchers are compelled to utilize indirect techniques for the parameterization of kinetic data. This discussion focuses exclusively on the concept of electrochemical reversibility, defined as the kinetics of electron transfer that occurs between the electrode and the analyte. In contrast, chemical reversibility pertains to the assessment of product stability during electron transfer reactions. A pivotal advancement was made by Nicholson in 1965, who, in his landmark paper, proposed a novel indirect approach for the approximate determination of the standard rate 

constant ( _k_[0] ) for a simple single electron transfer quasireversible couple (O + e ⇄ R).[2] Nicholson investigates the impact of _k_[0] on the shape of voltammograms in the context of kinetic analysis. He notes that a reduction in _k_[0] results in an increased separation of peak potentials between the cathodic and anodic peaks, represented as Δ _E_ p = _E_ pa − _E_ pc (where _E_ pc and _E_ pa denote the cathodic and anodic peak potentials, respectively). This widening of peak separation is proposed as a direct measure of the rate constant _k_[0] . The method has gained significant popularity due to its reliance on a single experimental parameter, Δ _E_ p.[3] Nicholson further demonstrated that Δ _E_ p correlates with a dimensionless kinetic parameter ( _ψ_ ), which reflects the balance between charge transfer and mass transport dynamics, ultimately influencing the overall characteristics of the cyclic voltammetry (CV) profile (eq 1).[4]

A particularly notable instance was recorded during the reduction of molecular oxygen (O2 + e[−] → O•−2 ) in room temperature ionic liquid (RTIL), where the diffusion coefficients _D_ O 2 and _D_ O•2 exhibit considerable differences in their magnitudes.[5][,][7] Nicholson was the pioneer in linking _ψ_ with Δ _E_ p. He analytically derived cyclic voltammograms (CVs) for quasireversible couples across a range of _ψ_ values from 20 to 0.1, under the premise that _α_ = 0.5, _T_ = 298 K, _D_ o = _D_ R, and switching potential, _Eλ_ of 141 mV relative to _E_ 0f ( _Eλ_ − _E_ 0f = 141 mV). He demonstrates that within the peak potential difference (Δ _E_ p) range of 61 to 212 mV, _ψ_ is solely a function of Δ _E_ p and is independent of experimentally variable parameters such as _Eλ_ and _α_ . The exclusion of _Eλ_ and _α_ from the calculations significantly simplifies the methodology and is strictly applicable for Δ _E_ p values within the range of 61− 212 mV. Next, He compiled a dataset of _ψ_ and Δ _E_ p (Table 1), suggesting that one can easily ascertain _ψ_ by consulting Table 1 in relation to their experimentally obtained Δ _E_ p at different scan rates, thereby facilitating the determination of _k_[0] through the application of eq 2.[2] Subsequently, in 1981, Heinze presented a similar _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p dataset (Table 1) through digital simulations, 

albeit with a greater number of data points.[8] The datasets provided by Nicholson and Heinze have consistently served as reliable references for determining _k_[0] for quasireversible couples over the years.[9] In 2013, Ichimura presented an additional dataset of _ψ_ vs. ΔEp values ranging from 62 to 929 mV.[10] However, all these datasets consist of discrete data points, prompting various researchers to independently fit either the Nicholson or the Heinze datasets, resulting in the proposal of several empirical equations [eq 3 − Parker (1980); eq 4 − Russel (1984); eq 5 − Lavagnini (2004); eq 6 − Swaddle (2005); eq 7 − Ikeda (2007); eq 8 − Guin (2018); eq 9 − Mckone (2019); and eq 10 − Ludwig (2021)] to ascertain _ψ_ for specific values of Δ _E_ p within the range of 61 to 212 mV.[11][−][14] Interestingly, some researchers have introduced empirical equations that do not conform to the Δ _E_ p range of 61 to 212 mV established by Nicholson. In 1995, Paul and Leddy performed a linear regression on the Nicholson dataset, focusing on a narrower range of Δ _E_ p from 73 to 145 mV, which led to the formulation of eq 11.[15] Vilela (2024) independently analyzed a derived _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p dataset, fitting it within the range of 98 < Δ _E_ p < 290 mV and presenting empirical eq 12.[16] Similarly, Anton and Munoz (2025) developed empirical eq 13, which is applicable for Δ _E_ p values greater than 198 mV.[17] In 1981, Klingler−Kochi established an equation aimed at the direct assessment of _k_[0] for Δ _E_ p greater than 150 mV, which requires knowledge of _α_ , unlike the simplified approach by Nicholson.[18] Recently, the − author has revised the Klingler Kochi equation.[6] Furthermore, in 2004, Lavagnini proposed a comprehensive approach that − merges the Nicholson and Klingler Kochi methods, applicable for all values of Δ _E_ p.[12] He indicated that eq 5 should be applied when Δ _E_ p is less than 200 mV, while eq 14 is designated for Δ _E_ p values above 200 mV. Equation 14 is derived by substituting _α_ = 0.5 into the Klingler−Kochi equation, thereby aligning it with Nicholson’s method, which does not necessitate the determination of _α_ .[19][,][20] This technique has become quite favored among researchers for calculating _k_[0] .

Among the 12 empirical equations considered, the Lavagnini equation (eq 5) stands out as the most widely accepted by researchers.[21] In contrast, the Nicholson approach is contingent upon several approximations, notably neglecting the effects of _Eλ_ and _α_ on Δ _E_ p.[2] Additionally, this method is susceptible to interference from uncompensated ohmic drop ( _iR_ u drop) and is only applicable within a specific range of Δ _E_ p, from 61 to 212 mV.[22] However, it is frequently observed in the literature that this method is utilized without accounting for potential drop, even in highly resistive environments, and is often applied for Δ _E_ p values exceeding 212 mV.[9][,][10][,][23][,][24] Nevertheless, to the best of my knowledge, the existence of three distinct _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p datasets and 12 various empirical equations linked to the same methodology introduces uncertainties and leaves numerous ambiguities unaddressed, such as 

- (i) Which _ψ_ vs. _E_ p dataset should be utilized? 

- (ii) Which equation among the 12 empirical equations should be selected for accurate kinetic analysis? 

- (iii) Which should be favored: the _ψ_ vs. _E_ p dataset or the 

empirical equation? 

- (iv) Is it feasible to use the empirical equations beyond the 

   - Δ _E_ p range set by Nicholson? 

- (v) If the current _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p dataset or empirical equations fail to deliver precise kinetic analysis, what strategies can enhance the accuracy of the Nicholson method? 

- (vi) What are the common assumptions and limitations of the Nicholson approach that researchers often neglect? 

- (vii) What impact do _Eλ_ , _α_ , and _T_ have on the kinetic analysis? 

- (viii) Is it necessary to conduct Ohmic drop compensation and background signal subtraction before implementing the Nicholson method? 

- (ix) What alternative methods exist in comparison to the Nicholson method? 

This viewpoint seeks to clarify the ambiguities previously mentioned, aims to rectify the limitations of the Nicholson approach, improve its accuracy, and create a more comprehensive _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p dataset, thereby eliminating the necessity for empirical equations. The perspective systematically addresses all previously posed questions in distinct sections, with assertions further validated by experimental determinations of _k_[0] for the r e d o x c o u p l e s [Ru(NH )3 6]3+/[Ru(NH )3 6]2+, [Fe(CN)6]3 /[Fe(CN)6]4 , and Eu[3+] /Eu[2+] . 

## ■ **[EXPERIMENTAL SECTION]** 

All simulations were performed based on the premise of a simple single electron transfer quasireversible redox couple, following Butler-Volmer kinetics, and assuming that only oxidized species were present at the start of the experiment. This setup utilized a planar macro-electrode with zero double layer capacitance and uncompensated ohmic resistances, functioning under conditions of semi-infinite linear diffusion. The simulations were carried out for a single cycle using DigiSim 3.03b (Bioanalytical Systems) at a temperature of 25 °C, with equal diffusion coefficients for both oxidized and reduced species. This approach disregards any contributions from chemical or nonelectrochemical processes.[1][,][6] Detailed experimental information can be found in the Supporting Information.

_**ψ**_ **vs. Δ** _**E**_ **p Dataset.** Table 1 presents the _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p dataset as reported by Nicholson and Heinze. However, a thorough analysis of the dataset reveals that lower values of Δ _E_ p (ranging from 61 to 65 mV) yield differing _ψ_ values when utilizing these two datasets. For instance, a Δ _E_ p of 61 mV corresponds to _ψ_ values of 20 and 11.5 when applying the Nicholson and Heinze datasets, respectively. The difference in _Eλ_ that generates these datasets is minimal ( _Eλ_ − _E_ 0f = 141 mV for Nicholson and 150 mV for Heinze), which cannot explain the substantial discrepancies observed. As a result, there is a lack of clarity regarding which data set provides the correct values of _ψ_ for a given value of Δ _E_ p. To resolve this lack of clarity, the Nicholson and Heinze data set has been theoretically reconstructed through digital simulations that mimic the conditions described by Nicholson and Heinze, with the findings summarized in Table 2. It is particularly noteworthy that a specific value of Δ _E_ p does not yield a unique value of _ψ_ ; rather, it generates a spectrum of _ψ_ values, with the average value of _ψ_ indicated in Table 2, which considers both extreme values and their standard deviation. For instance, under Nicholson conditions, the potential difference Δ _E_ p of 61 mV yields _ψ_ values ranging from 12 to 21, resulting in an average of 16.5 ± 4.5. A comparison of Tables 1 and 2 indicates that the conventional data sets from Nicholson and Heinze are somewhat deficient, while Table 2 offers a more precise _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p data set that provides consistent _ψ_ values for corresponding Δ _E_ p values across both data sets, thereby resolving any ambiguity. **Empirical Equations.** A total of 12 distinct empirical − equations (eq 3 eq 14) have been documented in the literature, derived from fitting the same _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p dataset (either the Nicholson or Heinze data set, as shown in Table 1). This 

abundance of equations may lead to confusion for newcomers in the field regarding which equation is most appropriate for calculating _ψ_ . To determine the reliability of these equations, _ψ_ values are derived using these equations for the corresponding Δ _E_ p values listed in the Nicholson dataset and are compared against the _ψ_ values documented in Table 1. The findings are illustrated in Figure S1, which indicates that certain equations yield precise values of _ψ_ at lower values of Δ _E_ p, while others do so at higher values of Δ _E_ p. However, none of the equations provide accurate values of _ψ_ throughout the entire spectrum of Δ _E_ p as claimed. A similar analysis was conducted using the Heinze dataset (Figure S2), leading to the same conclusion. Nevertheless, all equations exhibit a strong graphical correlation and demonstrate an excellent fit as reported in their respective publications.[9][,][12][,][13][,][15][,][16] In addition, the reliability of these empirical equations for kinetic analysis was assessed using simulation studies. Theoretical cyclic voltammograms (CVs) were produced at different scan rates (10 mV/s−100 V/s) for _k_[0] values between 1 and 0.001 cm/s. The electrochemical data of Δ _E_ p acquired for a designated _k_[0] value at various scan rates is utilized in these empirical equations to determine _ψ_ values at 1/2 varying _ν_ , and _ψ_ was plotted against ( _n D F_ o / _RT_ ) to derive the _k_[0] value. The analysis was confined to the validity range of Δ _E_ p for each empirical equation. Table 3 illustrates a comparison between the calculated and actual simulated values of _k_[0] , including the percentage error (% error). It is evident from Table 3 that none of the equations from eq 3 to eq 14 can accurately determine _k_[0] values across the full range of _k_[0] = 1− 0.001 cm/s, with some resulting in a significantly high % error. Equation 11 and eq 12 perform better in comparison to others (% error ≤ 10) but is applicable for a Δ _E_ p range different from that of Nicholson, which restricts their application. Therefore, it is demonstrated that, despite the existence of numerous empirical equations, none are appropriate for kinetic analysis across the Nicholson Δ _E_ p range.

The datasets consist of fixed data points, whereas empirical equations yield _ψ_ values across the full spectrum of Δ _E_ p ranging from 61 to 212 mV. However, if a _ψ_ vs. Δ _E_ p dataset could be developed for each mV interval of Δ _E_ p, it would eliminate the necessity for empirical equations, rendering the dataset selfsufficient for practical applications.

## ■ **[ASSUMPTIONS AND LIMITATIONS]** 

The Nicholson method relies on three fundamental assumptions, which are frequently overlooked by researchers when utilizing this approach.[2][,][12][,][14][,][19][,][24][,][25] The shapes of the voltammograms that determine Δ _E_ p are influenced by three critical factors: _Eλ_ , _α_ , and _ψ_ . Nicholson proposed the following assumptions: (i) Δ _E_ p is not influenced by _Eλ_ : _Eλ_ mainly influences the position of the anodic peak. When it is sufficiently separated from the cathodic peak (exceeding 90 mV), its effect on Δ _E_ p is minimal, allowing Δ _E_ p to be regarded as largely independent of _Eλ_ . However, this approximation is contingent upon the value of _ψ_ , with the accuracy improving significantly at lower _ψ_ values. For _ψ_ = 1, a variation in _Eλ_ from _Eλ_ − _E_ 0f of 100 to 300 mV results in only a 1% change in Δ _E_ p, while at _ψ_ = 20, the same variation leads to a 5% change in Δ _E_ p. (ii) Δ _E_ p is independent of _**α**_ : The parameter _α_ plays a crucial role in determining the symmetry of the potential energy barrier that 

separates reactants from products, with a theoretical range from 0 to 1; however, practical data generally restricts the values to between 0.3 and 0.7.[4][,][5] At _α_ = 0.5, the cathodic and anodic peaks are symmetrically positioned around _E_ 0f . For _α_ < 0.5, the height of the cathodic peak reduces and becomes increasingly broadened relative to the anodic peak and also causes a displacement of the cathodic peak’s position towards the negative side of the potential axis. In contrast, for _α_ > 0.5, a similar phenomenon occurs with the anodic peak. It is important to note that as the cathodic peak shifts towards the cathode for _α_ < 0.5, the anodic peak also undergoes a cathodic shift, leading to a consistent value of Δ _E_ p for _α_ values ranging from 0.3 to 0.7. Nonetheless, this remains an approximation that relies on the value of _ψ_ . Nicholson indicated that the accuracy of this approximation significantly improves for larger _ψ_ values, showing 5% and 20% deviations in Δ _E_ p at _ψ_ = 0.5 and 0.1, respectively, for _α_ values between 0.3 and 0.7.[2] (iii) Δ _E_ p is solely depending on _**ψ**_ : Given that Δ _E_ p is not influenced by _Eλ_ and _α_ , the shape of the voltammogram is solely determined by _ψ_ . These approximations are valid for Δ _E_ p values ranging from 61 to 212 mV. However, it is important to note that this method yields only approximate values of _k_[0] , rather than accurate measurements. Accurate determinations of _k_[0] can only be achieved when experiments are performed under the specified conditions of _T_ = 298 K, _Eλ_ − _E_ 0f = 0.250 mV and _α_ = 0.5, as outlined in Table 4. Minor deviations from the established parameters lead to slight uncertainties in the _k_[0] results;[26] however, the application should be confined to the Δ _E_ p range of 61−212 mV and temperatures around 298 K. The uncertainty in the _k_[0] results escalates as the temperature deviates from 298 K. It is noteworthy that Stangrit et al. propose an indirect approach to apply the Nicholson method at different temperatures by presuming a linear correlation between Δ _E_ p and _T_ ; however, this linear correlation is not strictly applicable.[27] Therefore, it is recommended that readers refrain from following these procedures and instead conduct experiments close to 298 K to ensure accurate results. A significant drawback of the Nicholson method is the occurrence of uncompensated ohmic drop, or _iR_ u drop, in voltammetric studies, which can have a pronounced impact. The influence of _iR_ u drop on the Δ _E_ p mirrors that of _k_[0] , making it a potential source of error in kinetic assessments. Therefore, it is essential to compensate for _iR_ u drop prior to utilizing the Nicholson method.[2][,][22][,][28] It is also crucial to acknowledge that _iR_ u similarly affects other alternate kinetic analysis method in conventional cyclic voltammetry and must be adjusted prior to application. A simple method to evaluate the influence of iRu drop is to repeat measurements across different concentrations of the analyte. If the calculated _k_[0] varies with concentration, it is likely that _iR_ u is significant and requires correction. Regrettably, it is frequently observed in the literature that researchers employ the Nicholson approach without addressing iRu compensation, and this incorrect practice should be addressed without delay. Double layer capacitance ( _C_ dl) has no effect on the Δ _E_ p values, thus resulting in no errors within the Nicholson approach. Nevertheless, it can complicate the assessment of other kinetic parameters, including the diffusion coefficient, and has an indirect effect on this technique. Consequently, it is prudent to implement _iR_ u compensation and the subtraction of blank signals (which contribute to _C_ dl) as routine practices in voltammetric kinetic analysis. Detailed instructions regarding _iR_ u drop compensation and blank signal subtraction are provided in the Supporting Information.[1]

## ■ **[ALTERNATE METHODS]** 

Three distinct approaches exist for the determination of _k_[0] in quasireversible couples, specifically the Nicholson method, the Klingler−Kochi method, and the Gileadi method. Further information regarding the Klingler−Kochi and Gileadi methods can be found out elsewhere.[6][,][29][,][30] Although these three methods are frequently compared in academic discussions,[31] it is important to recognize that their applicability is contingent upon various factors. As previously mentioned, the Nicholson method is appropriate for Δ _E_ p values ranging from 61 to 212 mV and does not necessitate prior knowledge of _α_ ; in contrast, the Klingler−Kochi method is suitable for Δ _E_ p values exceeding 150 mV, where _α_ must be established. The Gileadi method, being a graphical approach, requires a substantial variation in scan rates (from mV/s to MV/s) and also necessitates knowledge of _α_ . However, the practical use of the Gileadi method is constrained by the need for significant scan rate variations, which are often unfeasible with standard macro planar electrodes due to limitations imposed by convection and charging currents. The lower threshold of the scan rate is set to keep the experiment within a timeframe of 10 to 20 seconds, as after this period, convection-driven mass transport becomes significant. The upper threshold is influenced by the charging current and uncompensated resistance.[4][,][30] Additionally, it is crucial to acknowledge that commercial potentiostats often need careful setup for high-speed experiments (e.g., bandwidth, filtering, auto-ranging), which imposes practical limitations on the 

Gileadi method. Furthermore, the Gileadi method should not be confused with the similar technique proposed by Laviron, which is utilized for surface-confined species and is widely referred to as the Laviron method.[32] Furthermore, both the − Klingler Kochi and Gileadi methods require prior knowledge of _α_ , a requirement that does not apply to the Nicholson method. This inherent simplicity contributes to the Nicholson method’s greater popularity among the three and its widespread application in voltammetry. Consequently, in this analysis, it is not juxtaposed with the outcomes of the other methods. The best approach for extracting kinetic parameters involves fitting experimental CVs at varying scan rates through simulation software. This method utilizes the entire CV curve comprising thousands of data points, as opposed to the two specific data points ( _E_ pc and _E_ pa) used in the Nicholson method.[33] Therefore, CV fitting via digital simulations should be the preferred choice for kinetic analysis. Nevertheless, due to its straightforward nature, the Nicholson method remains more widely adopted among researchers for the kinetic analysis of quasireversible redox couples. It is advisable to compare experimental voltammograms with simulated voltammograms (DigiSim,[34] DigiElch,[35] and free python tool like FreeSim[36] ) produced from the identified kinetic parameters through the Nicholson method to validate the findings.
